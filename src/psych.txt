# p-si
Define conformity :: A change in a person's behaviour or opinions as a result of real or imagined pressure from a person or group.
Internalisation :: A deep type of conformity: the person genuinely accepts the group's norms. Change is both public and private and lasts even when the group is absent.
Compliance :: A superficial, temporary type of conformity: the person publicly goes along with the majority but privately disagrees. It stops when group pressure is removed.
Informational social influence (ISI) :: Conforming because we want to be right. In new or ambiguous situations we look to others who we think know better. A cognitive process that usually leads to internalisation.
Normative social influence (NSI) :: Conforming because we want to be liked and accepted, and fear rejection. An emotional process that usually leads to compliance.
Research support for ISI :: Lucas et al. (2006): students conformed more to wrong answers on difficult maths problems than on easy ones. People conform when unsure, as ISI predicts.
Research support for NSI :: Asch's interviews: some participants said they conformed because they felt self-conscious and feared disapproval. When answers were written privately, conformity fell (to about 12.5%).
Asch (1951) procedure :: 123 American male students judged which of three comparison lines matched a standard line. One naive participant sat with 6-8 confederates who gave the same wrong answer on 12 of 18 critical trials.
Asch (1951) findings :: Participants conformed on 36.8% of critical trials. 25% never conformed and 75% conformed at least once. In the control condition the error rate was under 1%.
Asch variable: group size :: Conformity rose with the size of the majority up to about 3 confederates (31.8%). Adding more made little further difference. With 1 confederate it was about 3%.
Asch variable: unanimity :: When one confederate gave the correct answer (or a different wrong answer), conformity fell to about a quarter of its usual level (5.5% with a truthful dissenter). Breaking unanimity frees the participant.
Asch variable: task difficulty :: When the lines were made more similar in length (harder task), conformity increased. ISI matters more when the situation is ambiguous.
Asch evaluation: temporal validity :: Perrin and Spencer (1980) repeated the study with UK engineering students and found just 1 conforming trial out of 396. The original result may reflect conformist 1950s (McCarthy-era) America.
Asch evaluation: artificial task :: Participants knew they were in a study (demand characteristics), the task was trivial with no consequences and the groups were strangers, so findings may not generalise to real life.
Asch evaluation: sample and ethics :: Only American men were tested, so findings may not apply to women or collectivist cultures (Smith & Bond 1998 found higher conformity there). Participants were deceived and may have been embarrassed.
Define obedience :: A form of social influence where a person follows a direct order from someone they see as an authority figure, who usually has the power to punish disobedience.
Milgram (1963) procedure :: 40 male volunteers acted as 'teacher' and were told to give increasing shocks (15V to 450V) to a 'learner' (a confederate) for wrong answers. The experimenter used four standard prods, ending with 'You have no other choice, you must go on'.
Milgram (1963) findings :: All 40 went to at least 300V and 65% (26 of 40) went to the full 450V. Many showed extreme tension: sweating, trembling, nervous laughter, and three had seizures. Students had predicted only about 3% would go to 450V.
Agentic state :: A mental state where we feel no personal responsibility for our actions because we believe we are acting for an authority figure, as their agent. The opposite is the autonomous state.
Agentic shift :: The shift from the autonomous state to the agentic state. It happens when we see someone as a legitimate authority above us in a social hierarchy.
Binding factors :: Features of a situation that let a person ignore or minimise the harm they are doing, reducing their moral strain. For example, blaming the victim or denying the damage.
Legitimacy of authority :: We obey people higher in a social hierarchy because society agrees their authority is legitimate. We learn this in childhood from parents and teachers. Some authority is destructive, used to order harmful behaviour.
Agentic state: supporting evidence :: Blass and Schmitt (2001) showed students a film of Milgram's study. They blamed the experimenter rather than the participant, and recognised his legitimate 'scientific' authority.
Agentic state: limitations :: It does not explain why 35% of Milgram's participants refused, or why 16 of 18 nurses in Rank and Jacobson (1977) refused to give an overdose. Mandel (1998) notes Reserve Police Battalion 101 killed civilians without direct orders.
Legitimacy of authority: cultural evidence :: Kilham and Mann (1974) found only 16% of Australian women went to the top voltage, while Mantell (1971) found 85% of German participants did. Different cultures view authority differently.
Milgram variable: proximity :: Teacher and learner in the same room: obedience fell from 65% to 40%. Touch proximity (forcing the learner's hand onto a plate): 30%. Experimenter giving orders by phone: 20.5%.
Milgram variable: location :: When the study moved from Yale University to a run-down office block in Bridgeport, obedience fell to 47.5%. Yale's prestige gave the experimenter more legitimacy.
Milgram variable: uniform :: When the experimenter in a grey lab coat was replaced by an 'ordinary member of the public' in everyday clothes, obedience fell to 20%.
Bickman (1974) :: A field experiment in New York. Confederates in a jacket and tie, a milkman's outfit or a security guard's uniform gave orders to passers-by. People were about twice as likely to obey the 'guard' as the man in the jacket and tie. Supports uniform.
Situational variables: evaluation :: Supported by Bickman's field study. Miranda et al. (1981) found over 90% obedience in Spanish students, so findings generalise across cultures. But Orne and Holland (1968) argued participants may have seen through the set-up, especially in the uniform variation.
Authoritarian Personality :: Adorno et al. (1950): a personality type prone to obedience. Features: exaggerated respect for authority, contempt for people of lower status, conventional attitudes, rigid black-and-white thinking and prejudice. Measured by the F-scale.
Origins of the Authoritarian Personality :: Harsh, strict parenting with conditional love and criticism. Hostility towards the parents cannot be expressed, so it is displaced onto weaker others (scapegoats). A psychodynamic explanation.
Authoritarian Personality: evaluation :: Support: Elms and Milgram (1966) found fully obedient participants scored higher on the F-scale. Limitations: it cannot explain obedience across whole societies (e.g. Nazi Germany); the research is correlational; the F-scale has acquiescence bias and only measures right-wing authoritarianism.
Resistance: social support :: Others who resist pressure help a person to resist. In conformity, a dissenter breaks unanimity (Asch: 5.5%). In obedience, a disobedient peer acts as a model (Milgram: obedience fell to 10% with two disobedient confederates).
Social support: evidence :: Allen and Levine (1971): conformity fell with a dissenter even when he wore thick glasses and clearly could not see. Gamson et al. (1982): 29 of 33 groups rebelled against an oil company's unjust demands.
Locus of control (LOC) :: Rotter (1966). Internals believe events result from their own behaviour, so they resist social influence more. Externals believe events are due to luck, fate or others, so they conform and obey more. LOC is a continuum.
Locus of control: evaluation :: Support: Holland (1967) found 37% of internals refused to go to the highest shock compared with 23% of externals. Against: Twenge et al. (2004) found Americans became more external over 40 years yet also more resistant to obedience.
Minority influence :: When a minority persuades others to adopt their beliefs or behaviour. It usually leads to internalisation, with public and private change.
Consistency (minority influence) :: The minority must be consistent: synchronic (all members say the same thing) and diachronic (they say the same thing over time). This makes the majority rethink their views.
Commitment (minority influence) :: Taking part in activities at personal cost or risk shows commitment. The augmentation principle: the majority pays more attention when the minority makes sacrifices.
Flexibility (minority influence) :: Nemeth (1986): rigid repetition can seem dogmatic, so the minority should balance consistency with flexibility and accept reasonable counter-arguments.
Snowball effect :: Over time more people switch from the majority to the minority position and the rate of conversion speeds up, until the minority becomes the majority. This is the snowball effect described by Moscovici.
Moscovici (1969) :: Groups of 6 (2 confederates) judged the colour of 36 blue slides. A consistent minority calling them 'green' got agreement on 8.42% of trials; an inconsistent minority on 1.25%; the control group 0.25%.
Minority influence: evaluation :: Support: Martin et al. (2003) found people who heard a minority message were less likely to change their view again later, suggesting deeper processing. Limitations: artificial tasks such as slide colours lack real-world relevance, and effects in research are small (about 8%).
# p-mem
Multi-store model (MSM) :: Atkinson and Shiffrin (1968): memory is three separate unitary stores: sensory register, short-term memory and long-term memory. Attention moves information from the SR to STM, maintenance rehearsal moves it from STM to LTM, and retrieval brings it back to STM.
Sensory register: coding, capacity, duration :: Coding: modality-specific (iconic for visual, echoic for sound). Capacity: very large. Duration: under half a second (iconic).
STM: coding, capacity, duration :: Coding: mainly acoustic (Baddeley 1966). Capacity: 7 ± 2 items (Miller 1956), or about 4 chunks (Cowan 2001). Duration: about 18 to 30 seconds without rehearsal (Peterson & Peterson 1959).
LTM: coding, capacity, duration :: Coding: mainly semantic (Baddeley 1966). Capacity: potentially unlimited. Duration: potentially a lifetime (Bahrick et al. 1975).
Baddeley (1966) coding :: Participants recalled lists of acoustically or semantically similar and dissimilar words. Immediate recall (STM) was worse for acoustically similar words; recall after 20 minutes (LTM) was worse for semantically similar words.
Jacobs (1887) :: Measured digit span to test STM capacity. Mean span was 9.3 digits and 7.3 letters.
Peterson and Peterson (1959) :: 24 students recalled consonant trigrams after counting backwards in threes for 3 to 18 seconds (to stop rehearsal). Recall was about 80% after 3 seconds and about 10% after 18 seconds.
Bahrick et al. (1975) :: 392 American graduates matched names to yearbook photos. Recall was about 90% within 15 years of graduating and about 70% after 48 years, showing LTM can last a very long time.
Chunking :: Grouping items into larger meaningful units to increase the capacity of STM (Miller 1956).
MSM: strengths :: Case studies such as HM and Clive Wearing: hippocampal damage stopped new LTMs forming while STM stayed intact, suggesting separate stores. Baddeley's research shows STM and LTM code differently.
MSM: limitations :: STM is not unitary: KF (Shallice & Warrington 1970) had poor verbal STM but normal visual STM. LTM is not unitary either. Rehearsal is not the only route into LTM (Craik & Watkins 1973). Much of the research used artificial materials.
Working memory model (WMM) :: Baddeley and Hitch (1974): a model of STM made of a central executive that controls slave systems: the phonological loop, the visuo-spatial sketchpad and (added in 2000) the episodic buffer.
Central executive :: Supervises the system and allocates attention and resources to the slave systems. It has very limited capacity and does not store information.
Phonological loop :: Deals with auditory information and keeps its order. The phonological store holds words you hear (inner ear); the articulatory process allows maintenance rehearsal (inner voice). Capacity is about 2 seconds of speech.
Visuo-spatial sketchpad :: Stores visual and spatial information (inner eye). Capacity about 3 to 4 objects. Logie (1995) divided it into the visual cache (form and colour) and the inner scribe (spatial arrangement).
Episodic buffer :: Added in 2000. A temporary store that integrates visual, spatial and verbal information from the other stores and links to LTM, keeping a sense of time sequence. Capacity about 4 chunks.
Word length effect :: People remember lists of short words better than lists of long words, supporting a phonological loop capacity of about 2 seconds of speech (Baddeley et al. 1975).
Dual-task performance :: Baddeley et al. (1975): doing two visual tasks at once was harder than doing a visual and a verbal task together. Two visual tasks compete for the visuo-spatial sketchpad, supporting separate slave systems.
WMM: evaluation :: Strengths: KF had impaired verbal but intact visual STM, supporting separate components; dual-task studies support it. Limitations: the central executive is vague and poorly defined; evidence from brain-damaged patients may not generalise.
Interference theory :: Forgetting occurs because two pieces of information conflict, causing one or both to be forgotten or distorted. It mainly explains forgetting in LTM and is worse when the memories are similar.
Proactive interference :: An older memory disrupts a newer one. Example: a teacher who has learned many names in the past struggles to remember the names of a new class.
Retroactive interference :: A newer memory disrupts an older one. Example: learning a new phone number makes it harder to recall the old one.
McGeoch and McDonald (1931) :: Participants learned a word list then a second list. Recall of the first list was worst when the second list was synonyms, showing interference is strongest when material is similar.
Interference: real-life evidence :: Baddeley and Hitch (1977): rugby players who had played more games over the season forgot more names of teams they had played. This is retroactive interference in a real setting.
Interference: limitations :: Many studies used artificial word lists. Tulving and Psotka (1971) found interference effects disappeared when cues were given, so interference may block access temporarily rather than erase memories.
Retrieval failure (cue-dependent forgetting) :: Forgetting because the cues present when we learned are absent when we try to recall (encoding specificity principle, Tulving 1983). Context-dependent forgetting involves external cues; state-dependent forgetting involves internal cues.
Godden and Baddeley (1975) :: Divers learned words on land or underwater and recalled them in the same or the other setting. Recall was about 40% lower when the environments did not match. Context-dependent forgetting.
Carter and Cassaday (1998) :: Participants took antihistamines (causing drowsiness). Recall was worse when their internal state at recall did not match their state at learning. State-dependent forgetting.
Retrieval failure: evaluation :: Strong research support with real uses (e.g. the cognitive interview). But Baddeley argued contexts must be very different (land vs underwater) for strong effects, and Godden and Baddeley (1980) found no context effect when memory was tested by recognition.
Eyewitness testimony (EWT) :: The ability of people to remember details of events, such as crimes or accidents, that they have seen. Accuracy can be affected by leading questions, post-event discussion and anxiety.
Loftus and Palmer (1974) experiment 1 :: 45 students watched film clips of car crashes and were asked 'About how fast were the cars going when they ___ each other?'. 'Smashed' gave a mean estimate of 40.5 mph; 'contacted' gave 31.8 mph.
Loftus and Palmer experiment 2 (broken glass) :: A week later, participants who heard 'smashed' were more likely to report seeing broken glass (16 of 50) than those who heard 'hit' (7 of 50) or the control group (6 of 50), though there was none. Supports the substitution explanation.
Response bias vs substitution explanation :: Response bias: the wording of the question does not change the memory, it only influences the answer chosen. Substitution: the wording changes the memory itself.
Post-event discussion :: When co-witnesses discuss an event, their memories can become contaminated as they combine information (memory conformity). Gabbert et al. (2003): 71% of witnesses who discussed a crime reported details they had not seen.
Anxiety harms EWT: weapon focus :: Johnson and Scott (1976): participants who saw a man holding a bloody knife identified him correctly 33% of the time, compared with 49% for those who saw a man holding a greasy pen. Attention narrows onto the weapon.
Anxiety improves EWT :: Yuille and Cutshall (1986): 13 witnesses to a real shooting in Canada gave accurate accounts 4 to 5 months later. Those who reported the highest stress were the most accurate (about 88% vs 75%).
Inverted-U explanation :: Deffenbacher (1983) applied the Yerkes-Dodson law to EWT: recall improves as anxiety rises up to an optimal point, then falls sharply.
EWT research: evaluation :: Lab studies such as Loftus and Palmer lack realism; Foster et al. (1994) found real witnesses are more accurate because their answers have consequences. The research has real value for police questioning. Older witnesses may be less accurate (Anastasi & Rhodes 2006).
Cognitive interview (CI) :: Fisher and Geiselman (1992): a police interviewing technique with four main parts: report everything, reinstate the context, reverse the order and change perspective.
Cognitive interview techniques explained :: Report everything: include trivial details as they may trigger other memories. Reinstate the context: mentally return to the scene, using context cues. Reverse the order: reduces reliance on schemas. Change perspective: describe events from another viewpoint.
Enhanced cognitive interview :: Fisher et al. (1987) added: knowing when to make eye contact, reducing the witness's anxiety, minimising distractions, getting the witness to speak slowly and asking open-ended questions.
Cognitive interview: evaluation :: Köhnken et al. (1999) meta-analysis of 50+ studies: the enhanced CI produced 34% more correct information than a standard interview, but also more incorrect information. It is time-consuming and needs training, so police often use only parts of it.
# p-att
Define attachment :: A close, two-way emotional bond between two people, especially an infant and caregiver, shown by proximity seeking, separation distress and secure base behaviour.
Lorenz (1935) :: Lorenz split a clutch of goose eggs: half hatched with the mother, half in an incubator where the first moving thing they saw was Lorenz. The incubator goslings followed Lorenz. This is imprinting, which happened within a critical period of a few hours.
Sexual imprinting :: Lorenz observed that birds that imprinted on humans later tried to court humans as adults.
Lorenz: evaluation :: Support: Regolin and Vallortigara (1995) found chicks imprinted on simple shapes. But birds' attachment differs from mammals' (more emotional), so generalising to humans is hard, and Guiton (1966) found imprinting effects on mating could be reversed.
Harlow (1958) :: 16 rhesus monkeys were reared with two wire 'mothers', one giving milk and one covered in cloth. They cuddled the cloth mother and ran to it when frightened, whichever one gave milk. Contact comfort matters more than food.
Harlow: later effects :: Monkeys reared with wire mothers became aggressive and less sociable, bred less and some neglected, attacked or killed their offspring. There seemed to be a critical period of about 90 days for a real mother.
Harlow: evaluation :: Real-world value: helps social workers understand risks in neglect and informs zoo breeding. Ethical issues: severe lasting harm to the monkeys. Monkeys are more similar to humans than geese, but generalisation is still debated.
Learning theory of attachment :: Dollard and Miller (1950), 'cupboard love'. Classical conditioning: food (UCS) brings pleasure (UCR); the caregiver (NS) is paired with food and becomes a CS producing pleasure. Operant conditioning: crying is reinforced by the caregiver's response, and the caregiver is negatively reinforced when crying stops. Attachment is a secondary drive.
Learning theory: evaluation :: Against: Harlow's monkeys preferred comfort to food, and Schaffer and Emerson (1964) found many babies' main attachment was not to the person who fed them. It ignores interactional synchrony. Conditioning may still play some part.
Bowlby's monotropic theory :: Attachment is innate and adaptive because it improves survival. Babies have social releasers (crying, smiling, cuteness) that trigger care. Monotropy means one attachment, usually to the mother, is more important than the others. Includes a critical period and an internal working model.
Law of continuity and law of accumulated separation :: Continuity: the more constant and predictable a child's care, the better the attachment. Accumulated separation: the effects of every separation add up, so 'the safest dose is a zero dose'.
Critical period (Bowlby) :: About the first 2 years (sometimes given as up to 2.5 years). If an attachment has not formed by then, the child will find it much harder to form one later. Later described as a sensitive period.
Internal working model (IWM) :: A mental representation of the child's first attachment relationship. It acts as a template for future relationships and affects how the child will later parent.
Bowlby: evaluation :: Support for social releasers: Brazelton et al. (1975) found babies became distressed when their caregivers ignored their signals. Support for the IWM: Bailey et al. (2007) found mothers' attachment to their own mothers predicted their babies' attachment type. Against monotropy: Schaffer and Emerson found many babies form multiple attachments at the same time.
Ainsworth's Strange Situation :: Ainsworth (1970): a controlled observation of 12-18 month olds in a novel room, with 8 episodes of about 3 minutes each. It measures proximity seeking, exploration and secure base behaviour, stranger anxiety, separation anxiety and reunion behaviour.
Secure attachment (Type B) :: About 60 to 75% of British infants. They explore happily using the caregiver as a secure base, show moderate stranger and separation anxiety and are easily comforted on reunion.
Insecure-avoidant attachment (Type A) :: About 20 to 25%. They explore freely but do not use the caregiver as a secure base, show little or no stranger or separation anxiety and little reaction on reunion.
Insecure-resistant attachment (Type C) :: About 3%. They explore less and seek more closeness, show strong stranger and separation anxiety and resist comfort on reunion.
Strange Situation: evaluation :: Strengths: high inter-observer reliability (Bick et al. 2012: 94% agreement) and predictive validity (secure infants have better later outcomes). Limitations: it may be culture-bound (Takahashi 1990: Japanese infants rarely separated from mothers showed extreme distress), and it missed a fourth type, disorganised (Main & Solomon 1986).
Van IJzendoorn and Kroonenberg (1988) :: A meta-analysis of 32 Strange Situation studies in 8 countries (1,990 children). Secure attachment was most common everywhere (75% in Britain, 50% in China). Insecure-resistant was least common overall. Variation within countries was 1.5 times greater than between countries.
Cultural variations: evaluation :: The large combined sample improves validity. But the Strange Situation is an imposed etic based on American norms, and countries are not cultures: Van IJzendoorn and Sagi (2001) found differences between Tokyo and rural Japan. Simonelli et al. (2014) found 50% secure and 36% avoidant in Italy, possibly due to more working mothers.
Bowlby's theory of maternal deprivation :: Bowlby (1951): prolonged emotional separation from the mother during the critical period (first 2.5 years) causes lasting damage to intellectual and emotional development, such as low IQ and affectionless psychopathy (no guilt or empathy).
Bowlby's 44 thieves study (1944) :: Of 44 teenage thieves, 14 were classed as affectionless psychopaths and 12 of those had experienced long separations from their mothers before age 2. Only 2 of a 44-person control group had such separations.
Maternal deprivation: evaluation :: Bowlby did the interviews and diagnoses himself, so bias is possible. Lewis (1954) studied 500 young people and found early separation did not predict criminality. Rutter distinguished deprivation (losing an existing bond) from privation (never forming one). Koluchová's (1976) twins recovered from severe privation, so the critical period may be a sensitive period.
Institutionalisation :: The effects of living in an institution such as an orphanage, where there is no consistent caregiver and little emotional care.
Rutter's English and Romanian Adoptees (ERA) study :: Rutter et al. (2011) followed 165 Romanian orphans adopted into British families, compared with 52 British adoptees. Those adopted after 6 months showed disinhibited attachment and lower IQs (mean 77 if adopted after 2). Those adopted before 6 months showed no lasting difference (mean IQ about 102).
Bucharest Early Intervention Project :: Zeanah et al. (2005): 95 children in Romanian institutions compared with 50 who had never been institutionalised. 74% of the controls were securely attached compared with 19% of the institutionalised group; 44% of the institutionalised group showed disinhibited attachment.
Effects of institutionalisation :: Disinhibited attachment (equally friendly to strangers and familiar people, attention-seeking), intellectual disability (lower IQ), and poor peer relationships. Effects may be reversed if children are adopted early, before about 6 months.
Institutionalisation research: evaluation :: Real-world impact: better care in institutions, key workers and early adoption. Romanian orphanages were extreme, so results may not generalise to other institutions. Earlier studies had confounding variables (e.g. trauma), which the Romanian studies reduced.
Early attachment and later relationships :: Via the internal working model: secure attachment leads to better friendships and adult relationships; avoidant attachment links to difficulty with intimacy; resistant attachment links to jealousy and control. It also affects parenting (intergenerational continuity).
Childhood relationships evidence :: Kerns (1994): securely attached infants tend to form the best-quality childhood friendships. Myron-Wilson and Smith (1998): secure children were unlikely to be involved in bullying, insecure-avoidant children were most likely to be victims and insecure-resistant children most likely to be bullies.
Hazan and Shaver (1987) :: A 'love quiz' in a US newspaper had 620 replies. 56% were secure, 25% avoidant and 19% resistant. Secure respondents had the longest relationships; avoidant respondents reported jealousy and fear of intimacy.
Early attachment and later relationships: evaluation :: Mixed evidence: Zimmermann et al. (2000) found infant attachment type did not predict adolescent attachment. Studies like Hazan and Shaver rely on retrospective self-report. Findings are correlational, so other factors (parenting style, genes) may explain both. The theory can be deterministic.
# p-clin
Statistical infrequency :: Behaviour may be a sign of mental ill health if it is numerically rare. Example: an IQ below 70 (about 2% of people) is one criterion for intellectual disability.
Statistical infrequency: evaluation :: Strength: useful in real-life assessment (e.g. scores on the Beck Depression Inventory). Limitations: some rare traits are desirable (IQ above 130), labelling someone abnormal can harm them, and not everyone with an unusual trait needs treatment.
Deviation from social or cultural norms :: Behaviour may be a sign of mental ill health if it breaks the unwritten rules of a society or culture. Example: antisocial personality disorder involves failing to follow lawful and normal behaviour. Norms differ between cultures and over time.
Deviation from norms: evaluation :: Strength: used in diagnosing conditions such as antisocial personality disorder. Limitations: cultural relativism (hearing voices is normal in some cultures), and it has been used to justify abuses of human rights (e.g. the historical 'diagnosis' of drapetomania in enslaved people).
Failure to function adequately (FFA) :: A person may have a mental health problem if they cannot cope with ordinary demands of life, such as hygiene, holding down a job or keeping relationships. Rosenhan and Seligman (1989) signs: not following interpersonal rules, severe personal distress, irrational or dangerous behaviour. Measured with scales such as the GAF.
FFA: evaluation :: Strength: it considers the patient's own experience of distress. Limitations: it is hard to separate failing to function from choosing an unusual lifestyle, and judging what counts as 'adequate' is subjective and culture-bound.
Deviation from ideal mental health :: Jahoda (1958): mental ill health is not meeting the criteria for ideal mental health, such as having no symptoms or distress, a rational self-perception, self-actualisation, coping with stress, a realistic view of the world, good self-esteem, independence and success in work and love.
Deviation from ideal mental health: evaluation :: Strength: a positive, comprehensive approach that focuses on what is needed for good mental health. Limitations: culture-bound (self-actualisation and independence are individualist Western values) and the standard is unrealistically high, since very few people meet every criterion.
Define phobia :: An anxiety disorder involving an irrational, persistent fear of an object or situation. Types include specific phobias, social anxiety and agoraphobia.
Phobias: behavioural characteristics :: Panic (crying, screaming, running away), avoidance (making an effort to avoid the phobic stimulus) and endurance (staying in its presence but with high anxiety).
Phobias: emotional characteristics :: Anxiety (long-lasting unpleasant arousal) and fear (an immediate, intense response). The emotional response is unreasonable and out of proportion to the danger.
Phobias: cognitive characteristics :: Selective attention to the phobic stimulus (hard to look away), irrational beliefs about it, and cognitive distortions (e.g. a spider is perceived as bigger or more aggressive than it is).
Define depression :: A mood disorder characterised by low mood and low energy. Types include major depressive disorder and persistent depressive disorder.
Depression: behavioural characteristics :: Changed activity levels (lethargy, or sometimes agitation), disrupted sleep (insomnia or hypersomnia) and eating (more or less appetite), and aggression or self-harm.
Depression: emotional characteristics :: Lowered mood (feeling worthless or empty), anger towards self or others, and lowered self-esteem (sometimes self-loathing).
Depression: cognitive characteristics :: Poor concentration, attending to and dwelling on the negative (and recalling unhappy events), and absolutist black-and-white thinking.
Define OCD :: Obsessive-compulsive disorder: an anxiety disorder characterised by obsessions (persistent, intrusive thoughts) and/or compulsions (repetitive behaviours).
OCD: behavioural characteristics :: Compulsions are repetitive (e.g. hand-washing, counting, checking), compulsions reduce anxiety, and avoidance of situations that trigger anxiety.
OCD: emotional characteristics :: Overwhelming anxiety and distress, accompanying depression (low mood), and guilt and disgust (e.g. over minor moral issues or dirt).
OCD: cognitive characteristics :: Obsessive thoughts (about 90% of patients have them), cognitive coping strategies (e.g. praying, meditating), and insight that the anxiety is excessive, while staying hypervigilant.
Two-process model :: Mowrer (1960): the behavioural explanation of phobias. A phobia is acquired through classical conditioning and maintained through operant conditioning.
Two-process model: acquisition :: Classical conditioning: a neutral stimulus is paired with an unconditioned stimulus that causes fear, so the NS becomes a conditioned stimulus that triggers fear. Little Albert (Watson & Rayner 1920): a white rat paired with a loud noise led to fear of the rat, which generalised to similar furry objects.
Two-process model: maintenance :: Operant conditioning through negative reinforcement: avoiding the phobic stimulus removes the unpleasant anxiety, so avoidance is reinforced and repeated. This keeps the phobia going.
Two-process model: evaluation :: Strength: it led to exposure therapies (SD and flooding). Limitations: it ignores the cognitive side of phobias (irrational beliefs), cannot explain phobias that begin without a frightening experience, and does not explain biological preparedness (Seligman 1971): we readily fear things that were dangerous to our ancestors, such as snakes, but not cars.
Systematic desensitisation (SD) :: A behavioural therapy (Wolpe 1958) that uses counterconditioning: the patient learns to associate the phobic stimulus with relaxation instead of fear. Based on reciprocal inhibition: you cannot be relaxed and afraid at the same time.
SD: the three steps :: 1) Anxiety hierarchy: patient and therapist list situations from least to most frightening. 2) Relaxation training: breathing exercises, muscle relaxation or meditation. 3) Exposure: the patient works up the hierarchy while relaxed, moving on only when calm at each level (in real life or imagined).
SD: evaluation :: Effective: Gilroy et al. (2003) treated 42 spider phobics; the SD group was less fearful than a relaxation-only group at 3 and 33 months. Suitable for people who cannot use cognitive therapies (e.g. some with learning disabilities). Acceptable: patients prefer it to flooding and fewer refuse it.
Flooding :: Immediate exposure to the phobic stimulus at full intensity, without a hierarchy, in one long session (2 to 3 hours) until anxiety subsides. The patient cannot avoid it, so the fear response is extinguished because the feared outcome never happens.
Flooding: evaluation :: Strength: cost-effective; it can work in one session and Ougrin (2011) found it as effective as other treatments and quicker. Limitations: very traumatic, so informed consent is vital and some patients quit, wasting time and money; less effective for complex phobias such as social phobia with strong cognitive aspects.
Beck's negative triad :: Beck (1967): depressed people have faulty information processing and negative self-schemas, and hold a negative triad of automatic thoughts about themselves, the world and the future.
Faulty information processing :: Attending to the negatives of a situation and ignoring the positives, black-and-white thinking, overgeneralising and catastrophising.
Negative self-schemas :: Packages of negative information about ourselves, often formed in childhood, which make us interpret all information about ourselves negatively.
Beck: evaluation :: Support: Grazioli and Terry (2000) found pregnant women with high cognitive vulnerability were more likely to develop postnatal depression; Cohen et al. (2019) found early cognitive vulnerability in 473 adolescents predicted later depression. Real-world use in CBT and screening. Limitations: it cannot explain every feature (e.g. anger, or rare delusions such as Cotard syndrome) and the link may be correlational.
Ellis's ABC model :: Ellis (1962): A is the activating event (a negative event), B is the belief about it (rational or irrational, e.g. 'musturbation', 'I-can't-stand-it-itis', utopianism), C is the consequence (rational beliefs lead to healthy emotions, irrational beliefs lead to depression).
Ellis: evaluation :: It explains reactive depression (with an obvious trigger) but not depression that has no clear trigger. It led to REBT. But it can be seen as blaming the patient, since it focuses on beliefs rather than real circumstances such as poverty or abuse.
CBT for depression :: Assessment, then identifying and challenging negative or irrational thoughts. Beck's cognitive therapy treats the patient as a scientist who tests the reality of their beliefs, often through homework. Ellis's REBT extends ABC to ABCDE: D is disputing irrational beliefs and E is the effect.
Ways to challenge irrational thoughts :: Empirical argument (what is the evidence?), logical argument (does the belief follow from the facts?), pragmatic argument (is the belief useful?). Behavioural activation encourages enjoyable activities.
CBT for depression: evaluation :: Effective: March et al. (2007) studied 327 depressed adolescents; after 36 weeks 81% improved with CBT, 81% with antidepressants and 86% with both. Limitations: it may not suit severe depression or people with low motivation; relapse is common (Ali et al. 2017: 42% relapsed within a year); it may neglect life circumstances; the therapist-client relationship may matter more than the technique.
Genetic explanation of OCD :: OCD is polygenic: Taylor (2013) found up to 230 candidate genes. Examples: the SERT gene (serotonin transporter) and the COMT gene (regulates dopamine). It is aetiologically heterogeneous: different combinations of genes cause OCD in different people.
Genetic explanation of OCD: evaluation :: Support: Nestadt et al. (2010) found 68% concordance for identical twins and 31% for non-identical twins; Lewis (1936) found 37% of OCD patients had a parent with OCD. Limitations: so many genes are involved that it has little predictive value, concordance is not 100%, and environmental risk factors matter (Cromer et al. 2007: over half of OCD patients had a traumatic past event).
Neural explanation of OCD :: Abnormal neurotransmitters: low serotonin and high dopamine. Abnormal brain circuits: the orbitofrontal cortex sends worry signals to the thalamus, which the caudate nucleus normally suppresses; if it is damaged, the worry signals keep firing. The parahippocampal gyrus (unpleasant emotions) is also implicated.
Neural explanation of OCD: evaluation :: Support: antidepressants that raise serotonin reduce OCD symptoms, and OCD occurs with other disorders that have biological causes (e.g. Parkinson's). Limitations: serotonin is also involved in depression, which is often co-morbid with OCD; and cause and effect are unclear (brain differences may result from OCD).
Drug therapy for OCD :: SSRIs such as fluoxetine (usually 20mg a day) block the reuptake of serotonin into the presynaptic neuron, so more serotonin stays in the synapse and keeps stimulating the postsynaptic neuron. They take 3 to 4 months to work. Alternatives include tricyclics (e.g. clomipramine). Often combined with CBT.
Drug therapy for OCD: evaluation :: Effective: Soomro et al. (2008) reviewed 17 studies and found SSRIs more effective than placebos. Cheap and not disruptive to daily life. Limitations: side effects (indigestion, blurred vision, loss of sex drive) lead some to stop; it treats symptoms not causes, so relapse can follow when stopping; drug company funding may bias research.
# p-app
Behaviourist approach: assumptions :: All behaviour is learned from the environment. Only observable behaviour should be studied, using lab experiments. The same basic processes of learning apply to animals and humans, so animals can be studied. We are born a blank slate (tabula rasa).
Classical conditioning :: Learning by association (Pavlov). Food (UCS) produces salivation (UCR). A bell (NS) is repeatedly paired with food. The bell becomes a CS that produces salivation (CR) on its own.
Operant conditioning :: Learning through consequences (Skinner). Positive reinforcement: a reward makes behaviour more likely. Negative reinforcement: escaping or avoiding something unpleasant makes behaviour more likely. Punishment: an unpleasant consequence makes behaviour less likely.
Skinner's research :: Rats in a Skinner box learned to press a lever when it delivered food (positive reinforcement) or switched off an electric shock (negative reinforcement).
Behaviourist approach: evaluation :: Strengths: scientific credibility (controlled, objective, replicable lab experiments) and real-world applications (token economies, systematic desensitisation). Limitations: environmental determinism ignores free will; it ignores cognition; there are ethical and practical issues with animal research and generalising to humans.
Social learning theory (SLT): assumptions :: Bandura: we learn directly through conditioning and indirectly by observing and imitating models. Learning involves vicarious reinforcement, identification and mediational (cognitive) processes.
Imitation :: Copying the behaviour of others.
Identification :: When an observer associates themselves with a role model and wants to be like them, often because the model is similar in age, gender or status.
Vicarious reinforcement :: Learning by observing the consequences of others' behaviour. Behaviour that is rewarded in a model is more likely to be imitated.
Mediational processes :: Cognitive factors between stimulus and response: attention (noticing the behaviour), retention (remembering it), motor reproduction (being able to perform it) and motivation (wanting to perform it). The first two concern learning, the last two performance.
Bandura et al. (1961) :: Children watched an adult behave aggressively (hitting with a mallet, shouting) or non-aggressively towards a Bobo doll. Those who saw the aggressive model were more aggressive later; boys were more aggressive than girls, and same-sex models were imitated more.
Bandura and Walters (1963) :: Children who saw an aggressive model being rewarded were more aggressive themselves than those who saw the model punished. Supports vicarious reinforcement.
SLT: evaluation :: Strengths: it includes cognitive factors, so it is more complete than behaviourism, and it explains cultural differences. Limitations: lab studies such as Bandura's have demand characteristics (a Bobo doll is designed to be hit); it underplays biological factors (e.g. testosterone); Bandura later described reciprocal determinism.
Cognitive approach: assumptions :: Internal mental processes can and should be studied scientifically. Because they are private, they are studied indirectly by making inferences from behaviour. It uses theoretical and computer models and the concept of schema.
Schema :: A mental framework of beliefs and expectations built from experience. Schemas let us process information quickly but can distort perception and memory. Babies are born with simple motor schemas such as grasping.
Theoretical models (cognitive approach) :: Simplified, often diagrammatic representations of mental processes based on research, such as the multi-store model of memory. Many follow the information processing approach: input, processing, output.
Computer models (cognitive approach) :: Comparing the mind to a computer, with a central processor, coding and stores. This analogy has been used to develop artificial intelligence.
Cognitive neuroscience :: The scientific study of how brain structures influence mental processes, often using brain scans (e.g. fMRI). Example: Tulving found episodic and semantic memory are linked to different sides of the prefrontal cortex.
Cognitive approach: evaluation :: Strengths: scientific, objective methods and real-world applications (AI, CBT). Limitations: machine reductionism ignores emotion and motivation; artificial stimuli in lab studies lack external validity; it is soft determinist.
Biological approach: assumptions :: Everything psychological is first biological. Behaviour has a genetic and neurochemical basis. The mind and body are one. Behaviour is a product of evolution.
Genotype and phenotype :: Genotype: a person's actual genetic make-up. Phenotype: the way genes are expressed in physical, behavioural and psychological characteristics, which is affected by the environment (e.g. PKU can be prevented by diet).
Twin studies :: Compare concordance rates for identical (MZ, 100% shared genes) and non-identical (DZ, about 50%) twins. Higher concordance in MZ twins suggests a genetic influence.
Evolution and behaviour :: Darwin's natural selection: genetically determined behaviours that help survival and reproduction are passed on to future generations, e.g. attachment behaviours.
Biological approach: evaluation :: Strengths: scientific methods (fMRI, EEG, drug trials) and real-world applications (psychoactive drugs). Limitations: causal conclusions are uncertain (a drug working does not prove the chemical caused the disorder); it is biologically determinist and reductionist.
Psychodynamic approach: assumptions :: Freud: the unconscious mind drives most behaviour; personality has three parts (id, ego and superego); defence mechanisms protect the ego; childhood psychosexual stages shape adult personality.
The unconscious (Freud) :: The part of the mind we are not aware of, which still drives much of our behaviour. It holds threatening memories that have been repressed and can show itself in dreams and slips of the tongue. The preconscious holds thoughts we can become aware of.
The id :: The primitive part of the personality, present at birth. It works on the pleasure principle, is entirely unconscious and demands immediate gratification.
The ego :: Works on the reality principle and develops at around age 2. It mediates between the id and superego, reducing conflict with defence mechanisms.
The superego :: Forms at the end of the phallic stage (around age 5). It is our internalised sense of right and wrong, based on the same-sex parent, and works on the morality principle, punishing the ego with guilt.
Defence mechanisms :: Unconscious strategies the ego uses to manage conflict between the id and superego. Repression: forcing a distressing memory out of the conscious mind. Denial: refusing to accept reality. Displacement: moving feelings from their real source onto a substitute target.
Psychosexual stages :: Oral (0-1, mouth, conflict over weaning), anal (1-3, toilet training), phallic (3-6, genitals, Oedipus or Electra complex), latency (6 to puberty, earlier conflicts repressed), genital (puberty onwards). Unresolved conflict leads to fixation in adulthood (e.g. oral fixation: smoking).
Oedipus complex :: In the phallic stage, boys desire their mother and see their father as a rival, then fear castration. They resolve it by identifying with their father and taking on his gender role and values. Freud's case study of Little Hans.
Psychodynamic approach: evaluation :: Strengths: huge explanatory power and influence, and it introduced psychotherapy. Limitations: it is based on case studies (e.g. Little Hans) that are subjective and hard to generalise; its concepts are unfalsifiable (Popper called it pseudoscience); it is psychically determinist.
Humanistic psychology: assumptions :: People have free will and are self-determining. It focuses on the person's subjective experience and the drive to self-actualise, and rejects scientific methods in favour of an idiographic approach. Key figures: Maslow and Rogers.
Maslow's hierarchy of needs :: From bottom to top: physiological needs, safety and security, love and belonging, self-esteem, self-actualisation. Lower deficiency needs must be met before a person can reach self-actualisation, a growth need.
Self-actualisation :: The innate drive to grow psychologically and fulfil one's full potential.
Congruence (Rogers) :: When a person's self-concept (how they see themselves) and ideal self (who they want to be) are similar. A big gap (incongruence) leads to low self-worth and blocks self-actualisation.
Conditions of worth :: Conditions that parents or others put on their love ('I will love you only if...'). They cause incongruence and low self-esteem. Rogers argued people need unconditional positive regard, which client-centred therapy provides along with genuineness and empathy.
Humanistic psychology: evaluation :: Strengths: it is holistic, not reductionist, and has an optimistic view of people. Limitations: limited application beyond counselling, its concepts are hard to test scientifically, and it may be culturally biased because self-actualisation and independence are individualist values.
Comparing approaches: determinism vs free will :: Behaviourist (environmental), biological (biological) and psychodynamic (psychic) are hard determinist. SLT and cognitive are soft determinist. Humanistic psychology argues for free will.
Comparing approaches: nature vs nurture :: Biological: nature. Behaviourist: nurture. SLT: mainly nurture. Cognitive: both (innate schemas refined by experience). Psychodynamic: both (innate drives shaped by childhood experience). Humanistic: mainly nurture (conditions of worth).
Comparing approaches: reductionism vs holism :: Behaviourist (stimulus-response reductionism), biological (biological reductionism), cognitive (machine reductionism) and psychodynamic are reductionist. SLT is less reductionist. Humanistic psychology is holistic.
Comparing approaches: idiographic vs nomothetic :: Humanistic psychology is idiographic; psychodynamic is mainly idiographic (case studies) but proposes general laws. Behaviourist, biological, cognitive and SLT are nomothetic.
# p-bio
Divisions of the nervous system :: Central nervous system (CNS): the brain and spinal cord. Peripheral nervous system (PNS): the somatic nervous system (voluntary movement and senses) and the autonomic nervous system (involuntary functions), which has sympathetic and parasympathetic branches.
The brain and spinal cord :: Brain: the centre of awareness; the cerebral cortex handles higher mental functions. Spinal cord: passes messages between the brain and PNS and controls simple reflexes.
Somatic nervous system :: Carries sensory information from receptors to the CNS and motor commands from the CNS to skeletal muscles. Controls voluntary movement and reflex arcs.
Autonomic nervous system :: Controls vital involuntary functions such as heart rate, digestion and breathing. The sympathetic branch increases arousal (fight or flight); the parasympathetic branch returns the body to rest ('rest and digest').
Sensory neuron :: Carries messages from sensory receptors to the CNS. Long dendrites and short axons.
Relay neuron :: Connects sensory neurons to motor neurons or other relay neurons. Found only in the CNS. Short dendrites and short axons.
Motor neuron :: Connects the CNS to effectors (muscles and glands). Short dendrites and long axons.
Structure of a neuron :: Cell body (contains the nucleus), dendrites (receive signals), axon (carries the impulse away from the cell body, covered by a myelin sheath with nodes of Ranvier that speed it up) and terminal buttons (communicate with the next neuron).
Electrical transmission :: At rest the inside of a neuron is negatively charged. When activated, the inside briefly becomes positive, creating an action potential: an electrical impulse that travels down the axon.
Synaptic transmission :: The impulse reaches the presynaptic terminal, which releases neurotransmitter from synaptic vesicles. It diffuses across the synaptic cleft and binds to receptors on the postsynaptic neuron, where the chemical message becomes electrical again. Leftover neurotransmitter is reabsorbed (reuptake) or broken down by enzymes. Transmission is one-way.
Neurotransmitters :: Chemicals that carry signals across the synapse. Each has a specific shape that fits its receptor. Examples: acetylcholine (muscle contraction), serotonin (mood), dopamine (reward and movement).
Excitation and inhibition :: Excitatory neurotransmitters (e.g. adrenaline, glutamate) make the postsynaptic neuron more positive and more likely to fire. Inhibitory neurotransmitters (e.g. serotonin, GABA) make it more negative and less likely to fire.
Summation :: Whether a postsynaptic neuron fires depends on the sum of all the excitatory and inhibitory signals it receives. If the net effect is excitatory, it fires.
Endocrine system :: A network of glands that release hormones directly into the bloodstream to reach target organs. It acts more slowly than the nervous system but its effects last longer. The pituitary is the 'master gland', controlled by the hypothalamus.
Glands and hormones :: Pituitary (ACTH, oxytocin), thyroid (thyroxine, metabolism), adrenal glands (adrenaline for fight or flight, cortisol for stress), pineal (melatonin), ovaries (oestrogen, progesterone), testes (testosterone).
Fight or flight response :: A stressor is detected and the hypothalamus activates the sympathetic nervous system. The adrenal medulla releases adrenaline into the blood, raising heart rate and breathing, dilating pupils, increasing sweating and muscle tension and slowing digestion. The parasympathetic system restores rest once the threat has passed.
Role of adrenaline :: A hormone from the adrenal medulla. It raises heart rate (more blood to muscles), raises breathing rate (more oxygen), dilates pupils and releases glucose for energy, preparing the body for immediate action.
Fight or flight: evaluation :: Beta bias: Taylor et al. (2000) suggested females 'tend and befriend' (linked to oxytocin). Gray (1988) noted a first 'freeze' response. In modern life the response can be maladaptive, with repeated activation linked to heart disease.
fMRI :: Functional magnetic resonance imaging detects changes in blood oxygen and flow linked to neural activity, producing 3D images of which areas are active. Strengths: no radiation, good spatial resolution (1-2 mm). Limitations: expensive, poor temporal resolution (about 5 seconds), measures blood flow rather than neural activity directly.
EEG :: Electroencephalogram: electrodes on the scalp record the brain's overall electrical activity as waves. Used to diagnose epilepsy and study sleep. Strengths: excellent temporal resolution (about 1 ms), cheap. Limitation: poor spatial resolution, so it cannot locate the source of activity.
ERPs :: Event-related potentials: brainwave responses to a specific stimulus, isolated from EEG data by averaging out background activity. Strengths: more specific than EEG, excellent temporal resolution. Limitations: lack of standardised methods between studies, and background noise must be removed.
Post-mortem examinations :: Studying a person's brain after death, often someone with a rare disorder, and comparing it with a typical brain (e.g. Broca's patient 'Tan'). Strengths: detailed study of deep brain areas; generated early theories. Limitations: cause and effect are unclear, and consent can be an issue.
Localisation of function :: The theory that specific areas of the brain are responsible for specific physical and psychological functions.
Motor centre :: The motor cortex in the frontal lobe of both hemispheres. It controls voluntary movement on the opposite side of the body.
Somatosensory centre :: The somatosensory cortex in the parietal lobe. It processes sensory information from the skin, such as touch, heat, pressure and pain.
Visual centre :: The visual cortex in the occipital lobe. Information from the right visual field goes to the left hemisphere and vice versa.
Auditory centre :: The auditory cortex in the temporal lobe. It analyses sounds and speech-based information.
Broca's area :: In the left frontal lobe, responsible for speech production. Damage causes Broca's aphasia: slow, effortful, non-fluent speech (e.g. Broca's patient 'Tan', 1861).
Wernicke's area :: In the left temporal lobe, responsible for understanding language. Damage causes Wernicke's aphasia: fluent but meaningless speech, often with made-up words, and difficulty understanding.
Localisation of function: evaluation :: Support: brain scans (Petersen et al. 1988 found Wernicke's area active when listening and Broca's when reading) and neurosurgery (Dougherty et al. 2002: cingulotomy for OCD). Against: Lashley (1950) removed 10-50% of rats' cortex and found no single area mattered more for maze learning (equipotentiality); language may be more widely distributed (Dick & Tremblay 2016).
Hemispheric lateralisation :: The idea that the two hemispheres are functionally different, with some functions mainly in one hemisphere. For example, language is usually in the left hemisphere and visual-spatial processing and face recognition in the right.
Split-brain research :: Sperry (1968) studied 11 people whose corpus callosum had been cut to treat epilepsy. An image shown to the right visual field (left hemisphere) could be described in words. An image shown to the left visual field (right hemisphere) could not be named but could be drawn or picked out with the left hand.
Split-brain research: evaluation :: Strength: highly standardised, controlled procedures. Limitations: small, unusual sample (all had severe epilepsy), confounding variables such as drug history, and lateralisation may change with age (Szaflarski et al. 2006).
Brain plasticity :: The brain's ability to change and adapt through experience and learning. Rarely used connections are deleted (synaptic pruning) and frequently used ones strengthened, throughout life.
Plasticity research :: Maguire et al. (2000): London taxi drivers had more grey matter in the posterior hippocampus than controls, and this was linked to how long they had driven. Draganski et al. (2006): medical students' brains changed during exam revision.
Functional recovery after trauma :: After damage, healthy areas can take over the functions of damaged areas. Mechanisms: axonal sprouting (new nerve endings connect to undamaged cells), denervation supersensitivity, recruitment of similar (homologous) areas in the opposite hemisphere, and unmasking of dormant synapses.
Plasticity and recovery: evaluation :: Real-world application in neurorehabilitation (e.g. constraint-induced movement therapy). Plasticity can be negative (e.g. phantom limb pain, Ramachandran & Hirstein 1998). It declines with age, though Bezzola et al. (2012) found golf training changed motor areas in 40-60 year olds. More education predicts better recovery (Schneider et al. 2014).
# p-rm
Aim vs hypothesis :: An aim is a general statement of what the researcher intends to investigate. A hypothesis is a precise, testable statement of the expected relationship between variables.
Directional hypothesis :: States the direction of the difference or relationship (e.g. 'more', 'fewer', 'positive correlation'). Used when previous research suggests what will happen. Analysed with a one-tailed test.
Non-directional hypothesis :: States that there will be a difference or relationship but not which way. Used when there is no previous research or findings conflict. Analysed with a two-tailed test.
Null hypothesis :: States there will be no difference or relationship between the variables. It is what the statistical test assesses.
Independent variable (IV) :: The variable the researcher manipulates (or that varies naturally) to see its effect on the DV.
Dependent variable (DV) :: The variable the researcher measures. Any change should be caused by the IV.
Extraneous variable :: Any variable other than the IV that might affect the DV if it is not controlled, e.g. noise or time of day.
Operationalisation :: Defining variables clearly in terms of how they will be measured, e.g. 'memory' becomes 'the number of words correctly recalled from a list of 20'.
Laboratory experiment :: Conducted in a controlled environment; the researcher manipulates the IV and records the DV. Strengths: high control, so cause and effect can be shown, and easy to replicate. Limitations: may lack ecological validity; demand characteristics.
Field experiment :: The IV is manipulated by the researcher in a natural, everyday setting. Strengths: higher mundane realism and more natural behaviour. Limitations: less control of extraneous variables; ethical issues (no consent, invasion of privacy).
Natural experiment :: The researcher takes advantage of an IV that varies naturally, not manipulated by them (e.g. children in orphanages). Strengths: allows research that would be unethical otherwise and often has high external validity. Limitations: the event may be rare, so hard to replicate; no random allocation.
Quasi-experiment :: The IV is a difference that already exists between people (e.g. age, gender, having a disorder). Strength: often carried out under controlled conditions. Limitation: no random allocation, so confounding variables are possible and causation is uncertain.
Repeated measures design :: The same participants take part in all conditions. Strengths: participant variables are controlled and fewer participants are needed. Limitations: order effects (practice, boredom, fatigue) and participants may guess the aim. Order effects are controlled by counterbalancing.
Independent groups design :: Different participants take part in each condition. Strengths: no order effects and less chance of guessing the aim. Limitations: participant variables may differ between groups, and more participants are needed. Controlled by random allocation.
Matched pairs design :: Participants are paired on key variables (e.g. IQ) and one of each pair goes into each condition. Strengths: reduces participant variables and has no order effects. Limitation: matching is time-consuming and never perfect.
Counterbalancing :: A way to control order effects in repeated measures. Half the participants do condition A then B and the other half do B then A (or every participant does A, B, B, A).
Random allocation :: Each participant has an equal chance of being placed in each condition, which controls participant variables in an independent groups design.
Standardisation :: Using exactly the same procedures and instructions for all participants, so differences in the DV are not caused by differences in procedure.
Randomisation :: Using chance to decide things such as the order of conditions or materials, to reduce researcher bias.
Control group :: A group that does not receive the IV or treatment, used as a baseline for comparison.
Demand characteristics :: Cues that reveal the aim of a study to participants, who may then change their behaviour to please the researcher or to spoil the results. Reduced by a single-blind procedure.
Investigator effects :: Any effect of the investigator's behaviour on the outcome (the DV), such as leading questions, body language or how participants are chosen. Reduced by a double-blind procedure.
Single-blind vs double-blind :: Single-blind: participants do not know the aim or which condition they are in. Double-blind: neither the participants nor the person running the study know, which also reduces investigator effects.
Target population vs sample :: The target population is the group the researcher wants to study and generalise to. A sample is a smaller group drawn from it to take part, which should be representative.
Random sampling :: Every member of the target population has an equal chance of being selected (e.g. a random number generator). Strength: free from researcher bias. Limitations: hard and time-consuming, needs a full list of the population, and people may refuse.
Systematic sampling :: Every nth person on a list of the target population is selected. Strength: avoids researcher bias. Limitation: may still be unrepresentative by chance.
Stratified sampling :: The sample reflects the proportions of sub-groups (strata) in the population, e.g. if 40% are under 30, 40% of the sample are; people are then picked randomly within each stratum. Strength: representative. Limitations: cannot reflect every way people differ; time-consuming.
Opportunity sampling :: Selecting whoever is available and willing at the time. Strengths: quick, easy and cheap. Limitations: unrepresentative, and researcher bias in who is approached.
Volunteer sampling :: Participants select themselves, e.g. by replying to an advert. Strength: easy, and participants are engaged. Limitation: volunteer bias, since volunteers may share traits such as being curious or keen to help.
Bias and generalisation in sampling :: Bias happens when some groups are over- or under-represented in a sample. This limits generalisation: how far findings can be applied to the target population.
Pilot study :: A small trial run before the main study to check procedures, materials and instructions work, so changes can be made before time and money are spent.
Naturalistic observation :: Behaviour is observed in the setting where it normally occurs, without interference. Strength: high external validity. Limitation: little control, so hard to replicate.
Controlled observation :: Behaviour is observed in a structured setting where some variables are controlled (e.g. the Strange Situation). Strength: easier to replicate. Limitation: the setting may be artificial.
Covert vs overt observation :: Covert: participants do not know they are being watched, so behaviour is natural, but this raises ethical issues. Overt: participants know, which is more ethical, but they may change their behaviour.
Participant vs non-participant observation :: Participant: the researcher joins the group being studied, gaining insight but risking 'going native' and losing objectivity. Non-participant: the researcher stays separate, which is more objective but gives less insight.
Behavioural categories :: Breaking a target behaviour into observable, measurable components, e.g. 'affection' becomes hugging, kissing and smiling. They should be clear, not overlap and cover all possible behaviours.
Event sampling :: Recording every time a target behaviour occurs. Strength: good for behaviours that happen rarely. Limitation: complex behaviours may be missed if too much happens at once.
Time sampling :: Recording behaviour at set intervals, e.g. every 30 seconds. Strength: reduces the number of observations. Limitation: behaviour outside the intervals is missed, so the record may be unrepresentative.
Inter-observer reliability :: The extent to which two or more observers agree. They observe the same behaviour separately using the same categories and their tallies are correlated; a correlation of +0.8 or more shows good reliability.
Questionnaires :: A set of written questions used to assess thoughts and experiences. Strengths: can collect a lot of data quickly and cheaply, without the researcher present. Limitations: social desirability bias and response bias (e.g. acquiescence bias).
Open vs closed questions :: Open questions have no fixed answers and produce qualitative data, which is rich but hard to analyse. Closed questions have fixed answers (e.g. Likert scales, rating scales) and produce quantitative data, which is easy to analyse but may lack depth.
Likert scale :: A closed question where respondents rate their agreement with a statement, usually on a 5-point scale from strongly agree to strongly disagree.
Structured interview :: Pre-set questions asked in a fixed order. Strength: easy to replicate and less interviewer bias. Limitation: the interviewer cannot follow up interesting answers.
Unstructured interview :: No fixed questions, just a topic, like a conversation; the interviewer can probe further. Strength: rich detail. Limitations: more interviewer bias, harder to analyse.
Designing interviews :: Use an interview schedule (a standard list of questions), a quiet setting, build rapport with neutral opening questions, and keep answers confidential.
Designing questionnaires :: Avoid jargon, emotive or leading questions, double-barrelled questions (two questions in one) and double negatives. Use filler questions to hide the aim and start with easy questions.
Correlation :: A technique that shows the strength and direction of an association between two co-variables. There is no IV or DV. Shown on a scattergram as positive, negative or zero.
Correlation vs experiment :: In an experiment the researcher manipulates an IV and measures its effect on a DV, so cause and effect can be shown. In a correlation, two co-variables are measured without manipulation, so causation cannot be shown because a third variable might explain the link.
Correlation: evaluation :: Strengths: quick and economical, can use existing data, and identifies relationships to test further. Limitations: no cause and effect, and intervening variables may explain the relationship.
Correlation coefficient :: A number from -1 to +1 showing the direction and strength of a correlation. +1 is a perfect positive correlation, -1 is a perfect negative correlation and 0 is no correlation. +0.2 is weak positive; -0.8 is strong negative.
Content analysis :: An indirect way of studying behaviour by analysing communications people produce, such as texts, TV or books. Coding puts material into categories and counts them (quantitative). Thematic analysis identifies recurring themes (qualitative).
Content analysis: evaluation :: Strengths: high external validity because it uses real communications, and few ethical issues because the material already exists. Limitations: material is studied out of context, so the researcher may misinterpret it, and thematic analysis can be subjective.
Case studies :: An in-depth study of one person, group, institution or event, often over a long period (e.g. HM). Mainly qualitative data from interviews and observations. Strengths: rich detail, and allow study of rare cases. Limitations: hard to generalise, subjective interpretation and reliance on memory.
Qualitative vs quantitative data :: Qualitative data are words (e.g. interview transcripts): rich and detailed but hard to analyse objectively. Quantitative data are numbers (e.g. reaction times): easy to analyse and more objective but may miss meaning.
Primary vs secondary data :: Primary data are collected by the researcher for this study, so they fit the aims but take effort. Secondary data were collected by someone else (e.g. government statistics), so they are cheap and quick but may be inaccurate or not fit the aims.
Meta-analysis :: Combining the results of many studies on a topic to reach an overall statistical conclusion (an effect size). Strength: a larger sample makes conclusions more generalisable. Limitation: publication bias (the file drawer problem), as non-significant studies often go unpublished.
Mean :: Add all the scores and divide by the number of scores. Strength: uses all the data, so it is the most sensitive. Limitation: distorted by extreme values. Suits interval data.
Median :: The middle value when scores are put in order (average the two middle values if there is an even number). Strength: not affected by extreme scores. Limitation: less sensitive. Suits ordinal data.
Mode :: The most frequent value. Strength: easy, and the only measure for nominal data. Limitation: crude, and there may be more than one mode.
Range :: The highest value minus the lowest value. Strength: easy to calculate. Limitation: affected by extreme values and says nothing about how data are spread around the mean.
Standard deviation :: The average spread of scores around the mean. A large SD means scores are widely spread; a small SD means they are close to the mean. Strength: more precise than the range as it uses all scores. Limitation: can be distorted by extreme values.
Calculating percentages :: Percentage = (part ÷ whole) × 100. Percentage change = ((new − old) ÷ old) × 100.
Bar charts vs histograms :: Bar charts show discrete categories (e.g. nominal data) with gaps between bars. Histograms show continuous data, with bars touching, equal-width intervals on the x-axis and no gaps.
Scattergrams :: Used for correlations. Each point represents a pair of values for the two co-variables.
Normal distribution :: A symmetrical bell-shaped curve where the mean, median and mode are all at the centre. Most scores are near the middle and the tails never touch the x-axis.
Positive skew :: Most scores are bunched at the low end, with a long tail to the right (e.g. a very hard test). Mode < median < mean, as the mean is pulled towards the tail.
Negative skew :: Most scores are bunched at the high end, with a long tail to the left (e.g. a very easy test). Mean < median < mode.
Nominal data :: Data in separate categories, such as favourite colour; each item fits only one category. Use the mode.
Ordinal data :: Data that can be ranked but where intervals are not equal, such as ratings on a scale of 1 to 10. Use the median.
Interval data :: Data measured on a scale with equal, precisely defined units, such as time or temperature. Use the mean and standard deviation.
Sign test: when to use :: Testing for a difference, with a related design (repeated measures or matched pairs) and nominal data (or data turned into + and − signs).
Sign test: calculation :: 1) Work out the difference between each pair of scores and record its sign (+ or −). 2) Discard any zero differences and reduce N. 3) S is the number of the less frequent sign. 4) The result is significant if S is equal to or less than the critical value.
Significance and probability :: Psychologists usually use p ≤ 0.05: there is a 5% or lower probability that the result is due to chance. A stricter p ≤ 0.01 is used when being wrong would be costly, such as in drug trials.
Critical value :: The value a test statistic must reach for the null hypothesis to be rejected. Found in a table using: one- or two-tailed test, N (or degrees of freedom) and the significance level.
Type I error :: A false positive: rejecting the null hypothesis when it is true, claiming an effect that is really due to chance. More likely with a lenient significance level (e.g. 10%).
Type II error :: A false negative: accepting the null hypothesis when it is false, missing a real effect. More likely with a strict significance level (e.g. 1%).
Choosing a statistical test :: Ask three questions: 1) Is it a test of difference or correlation? 2) If difference, is the design related (repeated measures or matched pairs) or unrelated (independent groups)? 3) What is the level of measurement: nominal, ordinal or interval?
Tests for nominal data :: Unrelated difference: Chi-Squared. Related difference: Sign test. Association: Chi-Squared.
Tests for ordinal data :: Unrelated difference: Mann-Whitney. Related difference: Wilcoxon. Correlation: Spearman's rho.
Tests for interval data :: Unrelated difference: unrelated t-test. Related difference: related t-test. Correlation: Pearson's r. These are parametric tests, which also need normally distributed data and similar variances.
Calculated vs critical value rule :: If the test name contains an R (Spearman's, Pearson's, Chi-Squared, unrelated and related t-tests), the calculated value must be greater than or equal to the critical value. For Sign test, Wilcoxon and Mann-Whitney it must be equal to or less than the critical value.
BPS code of ethics :: The British Psychological Society's code is built on four principles: respect, competence, responsibility and integrity. Ethics committees weigh costs against benefits before approving research.
Informed consent :: Participants should know the aims, procedures, their rights (including the right to withdraw) and how their data will be used before agreeing. Alternatives where full consent would spoil the study: presumptive, prior general or retrospective consent. Parental consent is needed for under-16s.
Deception :: Deliberately misleading participants or withholding information. Acceptable only if it causes no undue distress. Dealt with by a full debrief, revealing the true aim and offering the right to withdraw data.
Protection from harm :: Participants should face no more risk than in everyday life, physically or psychologically. Dealt with by reminding them of their right to withdraw, debriefing, and offering counselling if needed.
Privacy and confidentiality :: Participants have the right to control information about themselves, and personal data must be protected (Data Protection Act / GDPR). Dealt with by anonymity (using numbers or initials) and not recording identifying details.
Peer review :: Experts in the same field anonymously assess a piece of research before it is published, to check its quality. It is used to allocate research funding, validate the quality and relevance of research, and suggest improvements.
Peer review: evaluation :: Limitations: anonymity can let reviewers criticise rivals unfairly; publication bias favours positive, headline-grabbing findings; groundbreaking work may be rejected because it challenges established ideas; flawed findings remain once published.
Implications of research for the economy :: Research can benefit the economy. Attachment research shows fathers can be primary caregivers, so both parents can work flexibly. Research into mental health treatments (e.g. CBT, drugs) helps people return to work sooner, reducing the cost of absence.
Reliability :: How consistent a measure or test is: whether it produces the same results each time it is used.
Test-retest reliability :: Giving the same test to the same people on two occasions, with enough time between (e.g. two weeks). The scores are correlated and +0.8 or above shows good reliability.
Improving reliability :: Questionnaires: rewrite ambiguous questions, use more closed questions. Interviews: use the same trained interviewer and structured interviews. Experiments: standardised procedures. Observations: clearly operationalised, non-overlapping categories and trained observers.
Validity :: Whether a finding is genuine: whether a measure tests what it claims to (internal validity) and whether findings generalise beyond the study (external validity).
Face validity :: Whether a test appears, at face value, to measure what it claims to. Checked by looking at it or asking an expert.
Concurrent validity :: Whether a new measure gives similar results to an existing, well-established measure of the same thing. Participants take both and the scores are correlated; +0.8 or above suggests good concurrent validity.
Ecological validity :: How far findings can be generalised from the research setting to other settings, especially everyday life.
Temporal validity :: How far findings from a study stay true over time, e.g. whether Asch's 1950s findings apply today.
Improving validity :: Experiments: use a control group, standardised procedures and single- or double-blind designs. Questionnaires: anonymity and lie scales to reduce social desirability. Observations: covert observation and clear categories. Qualitative research: triangulation and direct quotes.
Objectivity and the empirical method :: Objectivity: researchers keep a critical distance so their personal views do not bias the data. The empirical method: knowledge is gained through direct observation and measurement, not argument or belief.
Replicability :: The extent to which other researchers can repeat a study's procedures and get the same results. It requires detailed reporting of methods.
Falsifiability :: Popper: a theory is only scientific if it could, in principle, be proved false. Scientific theories put themselves up for testing. Freud's theory is often criticised as unfalsifiable.
Theory construction and hypothesis testing :: A theory is a set of general principles that explain behaviour. It is built from evidence gathered through observation, and generates hypotheses that can be tested; the theory is then refined.
Paradigms and paradigm shifts :: Kuhn (1962): a paradigm is a set of shared assumptions and methods within a science. A paradigm shift is a scientific revolution where the dominant theory is replaced because contradictory evidence builds up. Kuhn argued psychology lacks a single paradigm (it is a 'pre-science').
Report section: abstract :: A short summary (150-200 words) at the start covering the aims, hypotheses, method, results and conclusions.
Report section: introduction :: A review of relevant theories and research, moving from broad to specific and leading logically to the aims and hypotheses.
Report section: method :: Enough detail for replication: design (with justification), sample (participants, sampling method, target population), materials, procedure (including standardised instructions, briefing and debriefing) and ethics.
Report section: results :: Descriptive statistics (tables, graphs, central tendency and dispersion) and inferential statistics (the test used, calculated and critical values, significance level and whether the null hypothesis is accepted or rejected). Raw data go in an appendix.
Report section: discussion :: Summarises the results in words, relates them to previous research, considers limitations and improvements, and discusses wider real-world implications.
Report section: referencing :: Full details of all sources cited. For a journal article: author(s), date, title, journal (in italics), volume and page numbers.
# p-id
Universality :: The aim to develop theories that apply to all people, regardless of gender or culture. Gender and cultural bias threaten universality.
Gender bias :: Representing or treating men and women differently in ways based on stereotypes rather than real differences.
Androcentrism :: Male-centred research: 'normal' behaviour is judged by a male standard, so female behaviour is seen as abnormal or inferior.
Alpha bias :: Exaggerating differences between men and women, often devaluing women. Example: Freud's claim that women have a weaker superego.
Beta bias :: Ignoring or minimising differences between men and women, often by studying only men and assuming the findings apply to women. Example: early fight or flight research (Taylor et al. 2000 proposed 'tend and befriend' in females).
Gender bias: evaluation :: Biased research can be used to justify discrimination (e.g. against women at work). Researchers have been mostly male. Feminist psychology aims to correct bias by studying women in real-life contexts and treating participants as collaborators (Worell 1992). Reflexivity means researchers acknowledge their own values.
Cultural bias :: Ignoring cultural differences and interpreting all behaviour through the lens of one's own culture, usually Western.
Ethnocentrism :: Judging other cultures by the standards of one's own, assuming it is superior. Example: the Strange Situation treats American-style secure attachment as ideal.
Cultural relativism :: The idea that norms, values and behaviour can only be properly understood within their own social and cultural context.
Etic, emic and imposed etic :: Etic: studying behaviour from outside a culture to find universal behaviours. Emic: studying from inside a culture to identify behaviours specific to it. Imposed etic: using a method developed in one culture to study another (e.g. the Strange Situation in Japan).
Cultural bias: evaluation :: The individualist-collectivist distinction may be outdated (Takano & Osaka 1999 found 14 of 15 studies showed no difference between the US and Japan). Some behaviours may be universal (e.g. facial expressions of emotion). Participants from other cultures may be unfamiliar with research, creating demand characteristics.
Free will :: The idea that humans can make their own choices and are not entirely controlled by biological or environmental forces (the humanistic view).
Hard determinism :: All behaviour is caused by internal or external forces beyond our control, so free will is impossible.
Soft determinism :: All behaviour has a cause, but people still have some conscious choice within certain limits (William James). The cognitive approach takes this view.
Biological determinism :: Behaviour is caused by biological factors we cannot control, such as genes, hormones and neurotransmitters.
Environmental determinism :: Behaviour is caused by features of the environment, such as reinforcement and punishment. Skinner said free will is an illusion.
Psychic determinism :: Behaviour is caused by unconscious conflicts we cannot control (Freud).
Scientific emphasis on causal explanations :: Science assumes every event has a cause and aims to discover general laws. Determinism fits the aims of science, as lab experiments manipulate an IV to find its effect.
Free will vs determinism: evaluation :: Free will has face validity, and an internal locus of control is linked to better mental health (Roberts et al. 1987). But Libet et al. (1983) found brain activity for a decision occurred before conscious awareness. Hard determinism fits science but not the legal system, which holds people responsible, and it is unfalsifiable.
Nature-nurture debate :: The debate over how far behaviour is caused by inherited characteristics (nature) or by environment and experience (nurture).
Heritability coefficient :: A number from 0 to 1 showing how far a characteristic is due to genes; 1.0 means entirely genetic. IQ is about 0.5.
Interactionist approach :: Nature and nurture interact. Examples: the diathesis-stress model (genetic vulnerability plus an environmental trigger); epigenetics (the environment changes how genes are expressed, which can be passed on); niche picking (people seek environments that suit their genetic tendencies).
Levels of environment :: Lerner (1986): the environment ranges from prenatal factors (e.g. a mother smoking) to postnatal experiences such as social conditions and culture.
Nature-nurture: evaluation :: Extreme nativist views have been linked to eugenics; empiricist views suggest any behaviour can be changed. Shared and non-shared environments make the two hard to separate. Plomin's idea of constructivism: people create their own nurture by their choices.
Holism :: The view that a system should be studied as a whole rather than as separate parts, because the whole is greater than the sum of its parts. Example: humanistic psychology.
Reductionism :: The view that behaviour is best explained by breaking it down into its simplest parts (the principle of parsimony).
Levels of explanation :: Behaviour can be explained at different levels, from the highest (social and cultural) to the lowest (biological and neurochemical). Example for OCD: cultural, psychological (thoughts), physical (compulsions), physiological (brain circuits), neurochemical (serotonin).
Biological reductionism :: Explaining behaviour in terms of neurons, neurotransmitters, hormones, brain structure or genes. Example: explaining OCD by low serotonin.
Environmental (stimulus-response) reductionism :: Explaining behaviour as simple learned links between stimulus and response, ignoring mental processes (behaviourism).
Holism vs reductionism: evaluation :: Holism: some behaviour only appears in groups (e.g. crowd behaviour), but holistic accounts are hard to test. Reductionism: scientific, allows variables to be operationalised and led to drug treatments, but it can oversimplify and ignore the meaning or context of behaviour.
Idiographic approach :: Studying individuals in depth as unique cases, usually with qualitative methods such as case studies and unstructured interviews. Used by humanistic psychology and Freud.
Nomothetic approach :: Studying large groups to establish general laws of behaviour, using quantitative methods and statistics. Used by behaviourist, biological and cognitive psychologists.
Idiographic vs nomothetic: evaluation :: Idiographic research is detailed and can generate hypotheses, but it is hard to generalise and less scientific. Nomothetic research is scientific and replicable but loses the individual person. The two can complement each other.
Social sensitivity :: Sieber and Stanley (1988): research with potential social consequences for participants or for the group of people they represent, such as research into race, sexuality or intelligence.
Social sensitivity: issues to consider :: Sieber and Stanley: implications (wider effects, e.g. justifying discrimination), uses (how findings may be used in public policy), validity (are findings sound? e.g. Burt's fraudulent IQ data) and how research questions are framed.
Social sensitivity: evaluation :: Socially sensitive research can benefit groups that are under-represented and challenge stereotypes, so it should not be avoided (Scarr 1988). But poor research has justified discrimination (e.g. Yerkes' army IQ tests influencing US immigration laws). Ethics committees weigh costs and benefits, but these are hard to predict.
# p-rel
Self-disclosure :: Revealing personal information to a partner. Social penetration theory (Altman & Taylor 1973): disclosure gradually increases in breadth then depth, like peeling an onion, and reciprocal disclosure builds intimacy.
Self-disclosure: evaluation :: Support: Sprecher and Hendrick (2004) found strong correlations between self-disclosure and satisfaction in dating couples. Limitations: the research is correlational, and there are cultural differences (Tang et al. 2013: Americans disclose more sexual thoughts than people in China, yet satisfaction is similar).
Physical attractiveness :: Important in initial attraction. Symmetrical faces are seen as attractive (a sign of genetic fitness) and baby-faced (neotenous) features trigger protectiveness. The halo effect: attractive people are assumed to have other good traits (Dion et al. 1972).
Matching hypothesis :: Walster et al. (1966): people choose partners of similar physical attractiveness to themselves, balancing the wish for an attractive partner against the fear of rejection, based on a realistic view of their own 'value'.
Physical attractiveness: evaluation :: Support for the halo effect: Palmer and Peterson (2012) found attractive people were rated as more politically knowledgeable. Cunningham et al. (1995) found similar attractiveness ratings across cultures. Against the matching hypothesis: Taylor et al. (2011) found online daters contacted people more attractive than themselves. Individual differences: Towhey (1979).
Filter theory :: Kerckhoff and Davis (1962): we narrow the 'field of availables' to the 'field of desirables' using three filters in turn: 1) social demography, 2) similarity in attitudes, 3) complementarity.
Filter 1: social demography :: Factors such as location, social class, education, ethnicity and religion. We are most likely to meet people who live nearby and are similar to us (homogamy).
Filter 2: similarity in attitudes :: Sharing core beliefs and values. Kerckhoff and Davis found this was most important for couples together less than 18 months.
Filter 3: complementarity :: Partners meet each other's needs by having traits the other lacks (e.g. one likes to care, the other to be cared for). More important for longer-term couples.
Filter theory: evaluation :: Support came from Kerckhoff and Davis's longitudinal study of student couples. Limitations: later research failed to replicate it (Levinger 1974); similarity may be a result of being together rather than a cause (Anderson et al. 2003); online dating weakens the role of social demography.
Social exchange theory (SET) :: Thibaut and Kelley (1959): an economic theory. People try to maximise rewards and minimise costs, and satisfaction depends on profit. The comparison level (CL) is the reward we think we deserve; the comparison level for alternatives (CLalt) asks whether we would be better off with someone else or alone.
Stages of social exchange :: Sampling (exploring rewards and costs), bargaining (negotiating exchanges), commitment (exchanges become stable) and institutionalisation (norms are established).
SET: evaluation :: Support: Kurdek (2005) found satisfaction was predicted by rewards and costs. Limitations: the direction of cause may be the other way (Argyle 1987: we only look at alternatives once dissatisfied); it ignores fairness and communal relationships where people do not keep score (Clark & Mills 1979); rewards and costs are hard to define; much research is artificial.
Equity theory :: Walster et al. (1978): satisfaction depends on fairness. Each partner's ratio of rewards to costs should be similar. Under-benefiting causes anger and over-benefiting causes guilt, and both lead to dissatisfaction. Perceived inequity motivates people to restore equity.
Equity theory: evaluation :: Support: Utne et al. (1984) found 118 newlywed couples who saw their relationship as equitable were more satisfied. Limitations: cultural differences (Aumer-Ryan et al. 2007: equity mattered less in Jamaica than the US); individual differences (Huseman et al. 1987: 'benevolents' tolerate under-benefit); Berg and McQuinn (1986) found rewards predicted satisfaction better than equity.
Rusbult's investment model :: Rusbult (1983): commitment depends on satisfaction level, comparison with alternatives and investment size. High satisfaction and investment with poor alternatives produce strong commitment, which keeps a relationship going.
Investment size :: Intrinsic investments: resources put directly into the relationship (time, money, self-disclosure). Extrinsic investments: things created by the relationship (children, shared friends, shared memories). Goodfriend and Agnew (2008) added planned future investments.
Relationship maintenance mechanisms :: Committed partners protect the relationship through accommodation (sacrifice and forgiveness), derogating attractive alternatives, positive illusions about their partner, and ridiculing alternatives.
Investment model: evaluation :: Support: Le and Agnew (2003) meta-analysed 52 studies (over 11,000 participants, 5 countries) and found all three factors predicted commitment. It explains why people stay in abusive relationships (Rusbult & Martz 1995: women in a shelter who returned to partners reported high investment and few alternatives). Limitations: relies on self-report; correlational.
Duck's phase model of relationship breakdown :: Duck: relationships break down through four phases, each starting at a threshold: intra-psychic, dyadic, social and grave-dressing.
Intra-psychic phase :: Threshold: 'I can't stand this any more'. One partner privately broods on their dissatisfaction, weighs up costs and alternatives, and may confide in a close friend.
Dyadic phase :: Threshold: 'I'd be justified in withdrawing'. The partners discuss their dissatisfaction, often arguing about fairness and costs. The relationship may be saved here or move on.
Social phase :: Threshold: 'I mean it'. The breakdown becomes public. Friends and family take sides and give advice, making it harder to go back.
Grave-dressing phase :: Threshold: 'It's now inevitable'. Each partner creates their own story of the relationship and why it ended, to protect their reputation and self-esteem and to move on.
Duck's model: evaluation :: Strength: it has practical uses, since interventions can target each phase (e.g. improving communication in the dyadic phase). Tashiro and Frazier (2003) found people grew from breakups, consistent with grave-dressing. Limitations: it describes rather than explains breakdown; it may be culturally biased (in collectivist cultures families are involved from the start); research with distressed participants raises ethical issues.
Online relationships: self-disclosure :: Reduced cues theory (Sproull & Kiesler 1986): online communication lacks non-verbal cues, causing de-individuation and less effective relationships. Hyperpersonal model (Walther 1996): online self-disclosure happens earlier and faster, creating intense intimacy that may end quickly ('boom and bust').
Online relationships: deception :: The hyperpersonal model says senders can use selective self-presentation, controlling how they come across. This allows deception, e.g. about age or appearance. Toma et al. (2008) found most online daters lied about at least one physical attribute, usually by a small amount.
Online relationships: absence of gating :: Gates are obstacles to forming face-to-face relationships, such as appearance, shyness or a stammer. Online, without these gates, people can focus on self-disclosure; McKenna and Bargh (1999) found shy and socially anxious people were more likely to form relationships online.
Online relationships: evaluation :: Support for the hyperpersonal model: Whitty and Joinson (2009) found online questions were more direct and probing. Against reduced cues theory: people use other cues online, such as emojis and timing of replies. Research quickly loses temporal validity as technology changes, and online and offline relationships are increasingly mixed.
Parasocial relationships (PSRs) :: One-sided relationships, usually with a celebrity or fictional character, where the other person is unaware the individual exists. More likely if the celebrity seems attractive, similar and accessible.
Levels of parasocial relationships :: McCutcheon et al.'s Celebrity Attitude Scale; Giles and Maltby (2006). Entertainment-social: interest for fun and gossip. Intense-personal: obsessive, compulsive feelings, e.g. seeing them as a soulmate. Borderline-pathological: uncontrollable fantasies and extreme behaviour, e.g. willing to do something illegal for them.
Absorption addiction model :: McCutcheon (2002): absorption means seeking fulfilment through a celebrity and becoming preoccupied with them. Addiction means needing a stronger 'fix', leading to more extreme behaviour and delusional thinking, such as stalking. It explains how PSRs can escalate.
Attachment theory explanation of PSRs :: Insecure-resistant people are most likely to form PSRs, because their unmet needs are satisfied without any risk of rejection. Insecure-avoidant people are least likely, because they avoid intimacy in all relationships.
PSRs: evaluation :: Support: Maltby et al. (2006) linked intense-personal celebrity worship with poor body image in teenage girls. Against attachment theory: McCutcheon et al. (2006) found no link between attachment type and PSRs in 299 participants. The absorption addiction model describes more than it explains. Most research is correlational self-report.
# p-sz
Define schizophrenia :: A severe mental disorder in which contact with reality and insight are impaired (a psychosis). It affects about 1% of people and is more often diagnosed in men, city dwellers and lower socioeconomic groups. Diagnosed with DSM-5 (a positive symptom is required) or ICD-11.
Positive symptoms of schizophrenia :: Additional experiences beyond the normal: hallucinations (sensory experiences with no basis in reality, such as hearing voices) and delusions (irrational beliefs, such as paranoia or delusions of grandeur).
Negative symptoms of schizophrenia :: The loss of normal abilities: speech poverty (reduced amount and quality of speech, with delayed responses) and avolition (severe loss of motivation for goal-directed activity).
Avolition: signs :: Andreasen (1982): poor hygiene and grooming, lack of persistence in work or education, and lack of energy.
Co-morbidity in diagnosis :: When two or more conditions occur together. Buckley et al. (2009): about 50% of people with schizophrenia also had depression and 47% substance abuse. High co-morbidity suggests schizophrenia may not be a single, valid condition.
Culture bias in diagnosis :: African-American and Afro-Caribbean people in the UK are several times more likely to be diagnosed than white people, but rates are not as high in Africa or the Caribbean. Hearing voices is acceptable in some cultures, and Escobar (2012) suggested white psychiatrists may over-interpret symptoms.
Gender bias in diagnosis :: Men are diagnosed more often than women. Women may function better (e.g. stronger relationships), so they may be under-diagnosed (Cotton et al. 2009). Broverman et al. (1970) found clinicians judged mental health differently in men and women.
Symptom overlap :: Schizophrenia shares symptoms with other disorders, such as bipolar disorder (delusions, avolition). Ellason and Ross (1995) found people with dissociative identity disorder had more schizophrenic symptoms than people diagnosed with schizophrenia. This threatens the validity of diagnosis.
Genetic explanation of schizophrenia :: Schizophrenia runs in families (Gottesman 1991: identical twin of a sufferer about 48% risk, non-identical twin 17%, general population 1%). It is polygenic; Ripke et al. (2014) found 108 genetic variations linked to it, including genes involved in dopamine.
Genetic explanation of schizophrenia: evaluation :: Support: family and twin studies, and adoption studies (Tienari et al. 2004). Limitations: concordance is not 100%, so environment matters (e.g. birth complications, cannabis, childhood trauma); families share environments as well as genes.
Dopamine hypothesis :: Original: excess dopamine (hyperdopaminergia) in subcortical areas such as the striatum, linked to positive symptoms; high dopamine in Broca's area is linked to auditory hallucinations. Revised (Davis et al. 1991): low dopamine (hypodopaminergia) in the prefrontal cortex, linked to negative symptoms.
Dopamine hypothesis: evaluation :: Support: amphetamines (which raise dopamine) worsen symptoms and can produce them in healthy people; antipsychotics that block dopamine reduce symptoms. Limitations: glutamate may be more important (Moghaddam & Javitt 2012); clozapine works partly through glutamate; the evidence is largely correlational.
Neural correlates of schizophrenia :: Patterns of brain structure or activity linked to symptoms. Negative symptoms: reduced activity in the ventral striatum (Juckel et al. 2006). Positive symptoms: Allen et al. (2007) found reduced activity in the superior temporal gyrus and anterior cingulate gyrus in patients who experienced hallucinations.
Neural correlates: evaluation :: Supported by scanning research. Limitations: correlational, so the brain differences may be an effect of schizophrenia or its treatment rather than a cause; some neural correlates (e.g. of avolition) are also found in depression.
Family dysfunction explanations :: Schizophrenogenic mother (Fromm-Reichmann 1948): cold, rejecting and controlling, creating distrust that leads to paranoid delusions. Double-bind theory (Bateson et al. 1972): contradictory messages make the world confusing. Expressed emotion: high criticism, hostility and over-involvement act as a stressor triggering onset or relapse.
Family dysfunction: evaluation :: Support: Read et al. (2005) reviewed 46 studies and found many hospitalised patients had a history of childhood abuse. Limitations: little evidence for the schizophrenogenic mother or double-bind; evidence is retrospective; the theories blame parents, which is socially sensitive and caused distress to families.
Cognitive explanations of schizophrenia :: Dysfunctional thought processing (Frith et al. 1992). Faulty metarepresentation (recognising thoughts as our own) leads to hearing voices and thought insertion. Faulty central control (suppressing automatic responses) leads to speech poverty and thought disorder, as words trigger unrelated associations.
Cognitive explanations: evaluation :: Support: Stirling et al. (2006) found patients took about twice as long on the Stroop task, showing poor central control. Limitation: it explains symptoms (proximal causes) but not their origins (distal causes), which may be biological.
Typical antipsychotics :: First-generation drugs from the 1950s, e.g. chlorpromazine. They are dopamine antagonists: they block dopamine (D2) receptors, reducing dopamine action and positive symptoms such as hallucinations. Chlorpromazine also sedates.
Atypical antipsychotics :: Second-generation drugs, e.g. clozapine and risperidone. They block dopamine more temporarily and also act on serotonin and glutamate receptors, aiming to treat negative symptoms and cause fewer side effects. Clozapine is used for treatment-resistant cases but needs regular blood tests (risk of agranulocytosis).
Antipsychotics: evaluation :: Effective: Thornley et al. (2003) found chlorpromazine improved functioning and reduced symptoms compared with placebo; Meltzer (2012) found clozapine helped 30-50% of treatment-resistant patients. Limitations: side effects (typicals: tardive dyskinesia; atypicals: weight gain); possible 'chemical cosh' misuse to calm patients; publication bias in drug research.
CBT for schizophrenia :: Usually 5 to 20 sessions. Patients learn how their irrational thoughts affect their feelings and behaviour, find explanations for their symptoms (normalisation) and test the reality of delusions. It helps people cope with symptoms rather than curing them.
CBT for schizophrenia: evaluation :: Support: Jauhar et al. (2014) meta-analysed 34 studies and found a small but significant effect on positive and negative symptoms. Limitations: the quality of CBT varies; access is limited (Haddock et al. 2014: about 10% of UK patients could get CBT); it may not suit very disorganised patients.
Family therapy for schizophrenia :: Therapy with the patient's family to reduce stress and expressed emotion, which can trigger relapse. Pharoah et al. (2010): it aims to reduce anger and guilt, improve communication and problem-solving, form a therapeutic alliance and help relatives balance caring with their own lives.
Family therapy: evaluation :: Effective: McFarlane (2016) found it moderately effective, with relapse rates reduced by about 50-60%. It benefits the whole family. Limitations: studies vary in quality; families may feel blamed.
Interactionist approach to schizophrenia :: Biological, psychological and social factors all contribute to schizophrenia. In treatment, drugs are combined with psychological therapies such as CBT or family therapy, which is standard practice in the UK.
Diathesis-stress model :: Both a vulnerability (diathesis) and a trigger (stressor) are needed. Meehl (1962): a single 'schizogene' plus a schizophrenogenic mother. Modern view: the diathesis is polygenic or psychological (e.g. early trauma), and stressors include cannabis use, family expressed emotion and urban living.
Interactionist approach: evaluation :: Support: Tienari et al. (2004) found adopted children of mothers with schizophrenia were more likely to develop it if raised in a critical, dysfunctional adoptive family. Tarrier et al. (2004) found drugs plus CBT worked better than drugs alone. Limitations: early versions oversimplified the diathesis; the way diathesis and stress interact is unclear.
# p-for
Offender profiling :: An investigative tool used by police to narrow down the likely suspects for a crime, based on analysis of the crime scene and evidence. Two approaches: top-down (typology, FBI) and bottom-up (data-driven, UK).
Typology (top-down) approach :: Developed by the FBI's Behavioural Science Unit. Profilers match crime scene evidence to pre-existing categories. Based on interviews with 36 sexually motivated murderers, offenders were classed as organised or disorganised.
Organised offender :: Evidence of planning; victim deliberately targeted; offender keeps control; few clues left; above-average IQ, skilled job, socially and sexually competent, often married with children.
Disorganised offender :: Little planning (impulsive); body usually left at the scene; little control during the crime; lower IQ, unskilled or unemployed, history of sexual dysfunction and failed relationships, often lives alone near the scene.
Constructing an FBI profile :: Four stages: 1) data assimilation (reviewing evidence such as photos and reports), 2) crime scene classification (organised or disorganised), 3) crime reconstruction (hypotheses about the sequence of events), 4) profile generation (hypotheses about the likely offender).
Typology approach: evaluation :: Limitations: it only applies to certain crimes (e.g. murder and rape, not burglary); it assumes behaviour is consistent across situations; the organised-disorganised split is too simple (Canter et al. 2004 analysed 100 US serial killings and found evidence for an organised type but not a distinct disorganised one); the original 36 interviews were unstructured and not representative.
Data-driven (bottom-up) approach :: Profilers work up from the evidence to hypotheses about the offender, without fixed categories, using statistical analysis of crime databases. Includes investigative psychology and geographical profiling. Associated with David Canter in the UK.
Investigative psychology :: Matching crime scene details to statistical patterns of previous offenders. Key ideas: interpersonal coherence (how offenders treat victims reflects how they behave in everyday life), forensic awareness (offenders who have been through the system hide evidence) and the significance of time and place.
Geographical profiling :: Using the locations of linked crimes to work out the likely home or base of the offender, based on spatial consistency. Canter's circle theory: marauders commit crimes close to home; commuters travel away from home. It also suggests the offender's mental map, transport and job.
Bottom-up approach: evaluation :: Support: Canter and Heritage (1990) used smallest space analysis on 66 sexual assaults and found common behaviours that could link cases; Lundrigan and Canter (2001) found serial killers' homes were usually central to body disposal sites. It applies to more crimes than the typology approach. Limitation: Copson (1995) surveyed 48 UK police forces and found profiling led to accurate identification in only about 3% of cases.
Genetic explanation of offending :: Offenders may inherit genes that predispose them to crime. Lange (1930): 10 of 13 identical co-twins of prisoners were also in prison, compared with 2 of 17 non-identical co-twins. Tiihonen et al. (2014) linked the MAOA gene and CDH13 gene to violent crime. Diathesis-stress: genes plus environmental triggers.
Genetic explanation of offending: evaluation :: Support: Crowe (1972) found adopted children whose biological mothers had a criminal record had a 50% risk of a record by 18, compared with 5% for controls. Mednick et al. (1984) studied 13,000+ Danish adoptees and found convictions were most likely when both biological and adoptive parents had convictions. Limitations: twins share environments; Lange judged zygosity by appearance; raises issues of responsibility.
Neural explanation of offending :: Brain differences in offenders. Many have antisocial personality disorder (APD), with reduced emotion and empathy. Raine et al. (2000) found an 11% reduction in prefrontal grey matter in people with APD. Keysers (2011) found criminals with psychopathy only activated their mirror neurons when told to empathise.
Neural explanation: evaluation :: Supported by brain scanning. Limitations: correlational, so brain differences may result from experience; Farrington et al. (2010) found offenders with APD also had many social risk factors; it is biologically reductionist and raises questions of responsibility.
Eysenck's theory of the criminal personality :: Eysenck (1947): personality has three dimensions: extraversion-introversion, neuroticism-stability and psychoticism. The criminal personality is a neurotic extravert with high psychoticism. Personality is biological and inherited.
Biological basis of Eysenck's theory :: Extraverts have an underactive nervous system, so they seek stimulation and risk, and do not condition easily. Neurotics have a highly reactive sympathetic nervous system, so they are anxious and unpredictable. Psychoticism involves coldness, aggression and lack of empathy.
Eysenck: the role of socialisation :: Criminal behaviour is developmentally immature: selfish and focused on immediate gratification. Children learn to delay gratification through conditioning, but people high in E and N condition poorly, so they are less likely to learn to control antisocial urges. Measured with the Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (EPQ).
Eysenck: evaluation :: Support: Eysenck and Eysenck (1977) found 2,070 male prisoners scored higher on E, N and P than 2,422 male controls. Limitations: Farrington et al. (1982) found offenders scored high on P but not on E or N; Bartol and Holanchock (1979) found Hispanic and African-American offenders were less extravert than controls (cultural differences); personality may not be stable across situations.
Moral reasoning and offending :: Kohlberg applied his stage theory of moral reasoning to crime. Offenders tend to reason at the preconventional level, where decisions are based on avoiding punishment and gaining rewards. Kohlberg et al. (1973) found violent youths reasoned at lower levels than non-violent youths.
Cognitive distortions :: Faulty thinking that lets offenders justify their behaviour. Hostile attribution bias: reading ambiguous cues as threatening (Schönenberg & Justye 2014: violent offenders saw ambiguous faces as angry). Minimalisation: downplaying the seriousness of a crime (Barbaree 1991: 54% of rapists denied the offence and 40% minimised the harm).
Cognitive explanations of offending: evaluation :: Support: Palmer and Hollin (1998) found 126 convicted offenders showed less mature moral reasoning than 332 non-offenders. Application: CBT challenges distortions (Harkins et al. 2010 found it reduced denial in sex offenders). Limitation: moral reasoning varies by crime type (Thornton & Reid 1982 found it applied more to crimes for financial gain than impulsive crimes).
Differential association theory :: Sutherland (1939): through interaction with others (family, friends), people learn the attitudes, motives and techniques for crime. A person offends when their pro-criminal attitudes outweigh their anti-criminal attitudes. It also explains reoffending, as prisoners learn from each other.
Differential association theory: evaluation :: Support: Osborne and West (1979) found 40% of sons of criminal fathers had a conviction by 18, compared with 13% of sons of non-criminal fathers. It moves the focus to social factors. Limitations: pro-criminal attitudes are hard to measure, it may stereotype people from deprived backgrounds, and the family link could also be genetic.
Aims of custodial sentencing :: Deterrence (prison is unpleasant, putting off the individual and society), incapacitation (protecting the public by removing the offender), retribution (society takes revenge in proportion to the crime) and rehabilitation (reforming offenders through education, training and treatment).
Psychological effects of custodial sentencing :: Stress and depression (suicide and self-harm rates are higher in prison), institutionalisation (adapting to prison routines so much that coping outside is hard) and prisonisation (adopting the inmate code, norms that are antisocial outside prison).
Custodial sentencing: evaluation :: Recidivism is high (Ministry of Justice figures suggest about 45% of UK adult prisoners reoffend within a year), and prison may act as a 'university for crime'. Effects vary by individual. It is hard to tell whether mental health problems were caused by prison or existed before.
Behaviour modification in custody :: Based on operant conditioning, using token economies. Desirable behaviours (e.g. following rules) are reinforced with tokens (secondary reinforcers) that are exchanged for rewards (primary reinforcers, such as phone calls or food). Undesirable behaviour loses tokens. All staff must apply it consistently.
Behaviour modification: evaluation :: Support: Hobbs and Holt (1976) found token economies improved behaviour in young offenders compared with a non-token group. It is cheap and easy to run. Limitations: changes may not last outside prison, where the rewards stop; it depends on consistent staff; some see it as manipulative.
Anger management :: A form of CBT. Novaco (1975) three stages: 1) cognitive preparation (identifying triggers and patterns of anger), 2) skill acquisition (cognitive, behavioural and physiological techniques, e.g. positive self-talk, assertiveness, relaxation), 3) application practice (role-play of provoking situations, with reinforcement).
Anger management: evaluation :: Support: Keen et al. (2000) found young offenders on a programme showed better awareness and self-control. It tackles causes of offending, unlike behaviour modification. Limitations: expensive and needs skilled staff; role-play may not match real situations; long-term effects on reoffending are unclear.
Restorative justice :: Focuses on rehabilitation through reconciliation. The offender meets the victim (face to face or indirectly, with a trained mediator) to discuss the harm caused. The offender takes responsibility and the victim has a voice. It may include restitution (e.g. repairing damage). It is voluntary.
Restorative justice: evaluation :: Support: the Restorative Justice Council (2015) reported 85% victim satisfaction, and a 2011 Ministry of Justice report found reoffending fell by about 14%. Limitations: offenders must accept guilt and may take part insincerely to get a lighter sentence; it can be distressing for victims; it is unsuitable for some crimes, such as domestic abuse.

Universality and bias :: Universality means a finding applies to all people regardless of gender or culture. Gender and cultural bias threaten universality, because theories based on one group (e.g. men, or Western individualist cultures) are wrongly treated as true of everyone.
Structuring an I and D essay :: For a 16-marker: explain the issue or debate clearly with key terms (AO1). Use examples from topics you have studied (e.g. attachment, approaches, schizophrenia). Evaluate: strengths, weaknesses, real-world implications, the interactionist or middle-ground position. Finish with a reasoned conclusion.
