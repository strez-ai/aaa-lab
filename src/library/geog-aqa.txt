# water | 3.1.1 | Water and carbon cycles: water
Systems terminology :: Inputs, outputs, stores, flows, boundaries and energy. Open systems exchange energy and matter; closed systems exchange energy only. Dynamic equilibrium and positive and negative feedback.
Global water stores :: Oceans hold about 97% of water; cryosphere about 2%; groundwater, lakes, soil moisture, atmosphere and biosphere hold the rest.
Processes driving the water cycle :: Evaporation, condensation (dew point, cloud formation), precipitation (orographic, frontal, convectional), cryospheric processes and interception.
Drainage basin system :: Inputs: precipitation. Stores: interception, surface, soil water, groundwater, channel. Flows: infiltration, throughfall, stemflow, percolation, throughflow, groundwater flow, overland flow. Outputs: evapotranspiration and runoff.
Water balance :: P = Q + E ± S (precipitation = runoff + evapotranspiration ± change in storage). Shows water surplus or deficit over a year.
Storm hydrographs :: Show discharge after a storm: rising limb, peak discharge, lag time and falling limb. Short lag times come from impermeable rocks, steep slopes and urbanisation.
Human impacts on the water cycle :: Deforestation increases runoff and reduces interception; urbanisation increases impermeable surfaces; farming and groundwater abstraction change stores.
Interception :: Precipitation caught by vegetation before reaching the ground; higher in summer and in coniferous forests.
Infiltration and percolation :: Infiltration is water soaking into the soil; percolation is water moving down from soil into rock. Infiltration capacity depends on soil type and saturation.
Overland flow :: Surface runoff when rainfall exceeds infiltration capacity (Hortonian) or when the ground is saturated.
Evapotranspiration :: Combined evaporation from surfaces and transpiration from plants. Higher with temperature, wind and low humidity.
Flood risk factors :: Heavy or prolonged rain, saturated ground, impermeable rock, steep slopes, deforestation and urbanisation.
Drought :: A long period of below-average precipitation. Meteorological, agricultural and hydrological drought; El Niño influences patterns.
Case study river catchment :: e.g. the River Exe or Eden: show how precipitation, land use and soils affect hydrographs and flooding.
Cryosphere and water :: Ice sheets and glaciers store water; melting raises sea levels and changes river regimes.
# carbon | 3.1.1 | Water and carbon cycles: carbon
Carbon stores :: Lithosphere (sedimentary rocks, fossil fuels) is the largest; also oceans, atmosphere, biosphere and cryosphere (permafrost).
Slow carbon cycle :: Carbon moves between rocks, oceans and atmosphere over millions of years: weathering, sedimentation and volcanic outgassing.
Fast carbon cycle :: Carbon exchange through photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition and combustion over days to centuries.
Biological and physical carbon pumps :: Phytoplankton take in CO2 and sink when they die (biological pump). Cold, dense water absorbs CO2 and sinks at high latitudes (physical pump).
Carbon budget :: The balance of carbon exchanged between stores. Burning fossil fuels and deforestation have increased atmospheric CO2 from about 280 ppm before industrialisation to over 420 ppm.
Links between water and carbon cycles :: Warming increases evaporation; ocean acidity rises as more CO2 dissolves; melting permafrost releases methane, a positive feedback.
Case study: tropical rainforest :: e.g. Amazon: huge carbon store with a fast cycle. Deforestation reduces interception and transpiration, increases runoff and releases carbon.
Mitigating carbon emissions :: Carbon capture and storage, afforestation, renewable energy, international agreements such as the Paris Agreement.
Photosynthesis and respiration :: Photosynthesis moves carbon from the atmosphere into biomass; respiration returns it.
Decomposition :: Decomposers release carbon to the atmosphere and soil; slower in cold or waterlogged conditions, forming peat.
Combustion :: Burning biomass and fossil fuels rapidly releases stored carbon into the atmosphere.
Carbon sequestration :: Capturing and storing carbon in soils, forests, oceans or geological formations.
Permafrost and carbon :: Permafrost stores huge amounts of carbon; thawing releases CO2 and methane.
Ocean acidification :: Oceans absorb CO2, forming carbonic acid; lower pH damages coral and shellfish.
Effects of rising carbon on climate :: Enhanced greenhouse effect, rising temperatures, changing precipitation and more extreme weather.
Case study: Arctic tundra :: Permafrost holds carbon; oil and gas extraction and warming threaten carbon and water stores. Management includes insulated pipelines and buildings on piles.
# coasts | 3.1.3 | Coastal systems and landscapes
Sediment cells :: Stretches of coast in which sediment is largely contained; there are 11 major cells around England and Wales.
Waves :: Constructive waves (strong swash, low frequency) build beaches. Destructive waves (strong backwash, high frequency) erode.
Erosion processes :: Hydraulic action, abrasion, attrition, corrosion (solution) and wave quarrying.
Erosional landforms :: Headlands and bays form on discordant coasts. Caves, arches, stacks and stumps form through erosion of weaknesses. Wave-cut platforms form as cliffs retreat.
Longshore drift :: Waves approach at an angle, carrying sediment up the beach in the swash; backwash takes it straight down, moving sediment along the coast.
Depositional landforms :: Beaches, spits (e.g. Spurn Head), bars, tombolos, salt marshes and sand dunes.
Sand dune succession :: Embryo dunes, fore dunes, yellow dunes, grey dunes, dune slacks and mature dunes, with plant succession (psammosere).
Sea level change :: Eustatic change: global change in sea volume. Isostatic change: land rises or falls. Emergent landforms: raised beaches. Submergent: rias and fjords.
Coastal management :: Hard engineering: sea walls, groynes, rock armour. Soft engineering: beach nourishment, managed retreat. Shoreline Management Plans and integrated coastal zone management.
Case study coast: Holderness :: One of Europe's fastest-eroding coastlines (about 2 m a year) due to soft boulder clay. Groynes at Mappleton protect the village but increase erosion further south.
Littoral zone :: The coastal zone from the backshore through the foreshore and nearshore to the offshore zone.
Sources of coastal energy :: Waves, tides and currents. Fetch and wind speed control wave energy.
Weathering on coasts :: Mechanical (freeze-thaw, salt crystallisation), chemical (carbonation of limestone) and biological weathering.
Mass movement :: Rockfalls, landslides, slumps and soil creep weaken and shape cliffs.
Concordant vs discordant coasts :: Concordant coasts have rock bands parallel to the coast (e.g. Lulworth Cove); discordant coasts have alternating rock bands at right angles.
Blowholes and geos :: Geos are narrow inlets eroded along joints; blowholes form when a cave roof collapses upwards.
Climate change and coasts :: Sea-level rise, more storms and greater erosion threaten coastal communities.
Case study: coastal LIC/MIC :: e.g. Bangladesh or the Maldives: vulnerable to sea-level rise and storm surges; management limited by cost.
# hazards | 3.1.5 | Hazards
Plate tectonics :: The Earth's structure, convection currents, ridge push and slab pull. Plate margins: constructive, destructive and conservative.
Volcanic hazards :: Lava flows, pyroclastic flows, lahars, tephra, gas eruptions and jökulhlaups. Measured by the VEI.
Seismic hazards :: Earthquakes produce ground shaking, liquefaction, landslides and tsunamis. Measured by the moment magnitude scale and Mercalli scale.
Park's model :: The hazard-response curve showing how quality of life falls during a disaster and recovers through relief, rehabilitation and reconstruction.
Hazard management cycle :: Mitigation, preparedness, response and recovery.
Degg's model :: A disaster occurs when a hazard affects a vulnerable population.
Tropical storms :: Form over oceans above 26.5 °C, between 5° and 30° from the equator. Hazards: high winds, storm surges, coastal flooding and landslides.
Wildfires :: Need fuel, dry conditions and ignition. Impacts on people and ecosystems; managed by controlled burns and firebreaks.
Multi-hazardous environment :: A place facing several hazards, e.g. the Philippines (typhoons, earthquakes, volcanoes).
Prediction and prevention :: Monitoring (seismometers, tiltmeters, gas sensors), land-use planning, building design, warning systems and education.
Hazard perception :: How people view a hazard depends on wealth, religion, education and experience; ranges from fatalism to adaptation.
Volcano case study :: e.g. Eyjafjallajökull 2010: ash cloud disrupted air travel across Europe, costing airlines millions; little loss of life in Iceland.
Earthquake case study :: e.g. Haiti 2010: magnitude 7.0, over 200,000 deaths due to poverty and poor buildings. Compare with Christchurch 2011.
Tropical storm case study :: e.g. Typhoon Haiyan 2013 in the Philippines: storm surge killed over 6,000 people.
Tsunami :: Caused by underwater earthquakes displacing water; e.g. Japan 2011 (Tohoku) caused nuclear disaster at Fukushima.
Wildfire case study :: e.g. Australia 2019-20 bushfires burned millions of hectares and killed billions of animals.
# global | 3.2.1 | Global systems and global governance
Globalisation :: The growing interdependence of economies, cultures and people through trade, investment, technology and migration.
Dimensions of globalisation :: Flows of capital, labour, products and services, and information.
Factors in globalisation :: Financial, technological (internet, transport), political (free trade, WTO) and the role of TNCs.
TNCs :: Transnational corporations spread production across countries for lower costs and access to markets, e.g. Apple or Unilever.
Global trade and unequal power :: Trade agreements and tariffs favour some countries; many LICs depend on primary products with volatile prices.
Global governance :: Rules, norms and institutions such as the UN, WTO, IMF and World Bank that regulate global systems.
The global commons :: Areas beyond national control such as the oceans, atmosphere and Antarctica.
Antarctica as a global commons :: Threats from climate change, fishing, whaling and tourism. Protected by the Antarctic Treaty, CCAMLR and IWC.
Impacts of globalisation :: Economic growth and development but also inequality, cultural homogenisation and environmental damage.
World Trade Organization :: Promotes free trade by reducing barriers and settling disputes; critics say it favours rich countries.
IMF and World Bank :: The IMF provides loans and stability; the World Bank funds development. Structural adjustment conditions have been criticised.
Unequal flows of trade :: Developed countries export high-value goods; many developing countries export low-value raw materials.
Global marketing and products :: Global brands spread consumer culture; supply chains span many countries.
Case study of a commodity :: e.g. smartphones or coffee: production, trade and global value chains.
Interdependence :: Countries rely on each other for trade, finance and migration, which brings both opportunities and risks.
Global governance issues :: Difficulties in governing climate change, migration and the global commons due to national interests.
# places | 3.2.2 | Changing places
Place and space :: Place is a location given meaning by people; space is a location without meaning.
Insider and outsider perspectives :: Insiders know and feel part of a place; outsiders see it from outside, often more objectively.
Endogenous and exogenous factors :: Endogenous: inside factors such as location, topography and demographics. Exogenous: outside relationships such as flows of people, money and ideas.
Experienced places :: Near places (local) and far places (distant but known through media).
Meaning and representation :: Places are represented in different ways, e.g. in media, art, music, statistics and maps.
Rebranding :: Reimaging and regeneration to change a place's identity and attract investment, e.g. tourism-led or sport-led.
Qualitative and quantitative sources :: Census data, geospatial data, photographs, interviews and art help study places.
Case study requirement :: Study your local place and a contrasting place to explore how relationships and meanings change.
Place identity :: Shaped by physical and human features, history, culture and the people who live there.
Changing demographics :: Migration, ageing and gentrification change a place's character and identity.
Economic change and place :: Deindustrialisation and globalisation change jobs and identity (e.g. former mining towns).
Social inequalities in places :: Differences in income, health and education between and within places.
Fieldwork methods :: Interviews, questionnaires, photographs, environmental quality surveys and census data.
# urban | 3.2.3 | Contemporary urban environments
Urbanisation :: Growth in the proportion of people living in urban areas, driven by rural-urban migration and natural increase.
Suburbanisation, counter-urbanisation and re-urbanisation :: Movements of people from cities to suburbs, to rural areas, and back into city centres.
Megacities and world cities :: Megacities have over 10 million people. World cities like London have global influence.
Urban forms :: Post-industrial cities, mixed-use developments, gated communities and edge cities.
Urban heat island :: Cities are warmer than surrounding areas because of heat-absorbing surfaces, waste heat and less evapotranspiration.
Urban drainage :: Impermeable surfaces increase runoff and flood risk. Sustainable urban drainage systems (SUDS) reduce it.
Urban waste and pollution :: Air pollution from traffic and industry (smog), waste disposal, and environmental problems in growing cities.
Sustainable cities :: Reducing ecological footprint through public transport, green spaces, renewable energy and recycling.
Urban social issues :: Inequality, deprivation and segregation in cities; regeneration aims to reduce these.
Case study contrasting cities :: Compare two cities (e.g. London and Mumbai) in terms of growth, inequality and urban policy.
Urban regeneration :: Improving run-down areas through redevelopment, e.g. London Docklands or King's Cross.
Gentrification :: Wealthier people move into poorer areas, improving housing but displacing existing residents.
Cultural diversity in cities :: Migration creates diverse cultural areas but can lead to segregation.
Urban microclimate :: Cities have higher temperatures, more rainfall and thunderstorms, poorer air quality and wind tunnels between tall buildings.
Urban form in LICs :: Rapid growth leads to informal settlements (slums), e.g. Dharavi in Mumbai.
Waste management :: Landfill, incineration, recycling and reduction strategies; LICs often lack formal systems.
Urban sustainability measures :: Ecological footprint, green belts, brownfield development and public transport.
Urban environmental problems :: Air pollution, water pollution, dereliction and loss of green space.
