# ancient | Philosophy | Ancient philosophical influences
Plato's analogy of the cave :: Prisoners see only shadows (the world of appearances); the escaped prisoner sees the sun (the Form of the Good). The philosopher should return to educate others.
Plato's theory of Forms :: Perfect, unchanging Forms exist in a separate realm and are known through reason; the physical world contains imperfect copies.
Aristotle's four causes :: Material, efficient, formal and final causes. The final cause is the purpose (telos) of something.
Aristotle's Prime Mover :: An unmoved mover that causes motion by attraction as a final cause; eternal, immaterial and perfect.
Plato vs Aristotle :: Plato trusted reason over the senses (rationalism); Aristotle trusted empirical observation (empiricism).
The Form of the Good :: The highest Form, which illuminates all others, as the sun lets us see objects in the cave analogy.
Criticisms of Plato's Forms :: Aristotle's third man argument; the Forms can't be proved; is there a Form of mud or hair?
Criticisms of Aristotle's causes :: The final cause may be a human projection; science explains things without purpose (Darwin, Dawkins).
# soul | Philosophy | Soul, mind and body
Plato's dualism :: The soul is immortal and belongs to the world of Forms; the body is a prison. Tripartite soul: reason, spirit and appetite.
Aristotle on the soul :: The soul is the form of the body, not separate from it; it cannot survive death.
Descartes' substance dualism :: The mind (thinking substance) is distinct from the body (extended substance): 'I think, therefore I am'.
Materialism :: Gilbert Ryle called dualism a 'category mistake' and 'the ghost in the machine'. Dawkins: humans are 'survival machines' for genes.
Plato's chariot analogy :: The charioteer (reason) controls two horses: spirit (noble) and appetite (unruly).
Aristotle's wax and seal :: Like the impression in wax, the soul is inseparable from the body.
Interactionism problem :: How can a non-physical mind affect a physical body? Descartes suggested the pineal gland.
Mind-brain identity theory :: Mental states are identical to brain states; criticised by qualia (what it is like to experience).
# design | Philosophy | Arguments based on observation
Aquinas' Five Ways :: Includes arguments from motion, causation, contingency and design (the fifth way: the arrow and archer analogy).
Paley's watchmaker :: The complexity and purpose of a watch imply a designer; similarly, the universe and the eye imply God.
Hume's criticisms of design :: The analogy is weak; the universe could have come from chance (Epicurean hypothesis); the designer could be many gods or imperfect.
Cosmological argument :: Everything has a cause, so there must be an uncaused first cause (God). Kalam version: the universe began to exist.
Darwin and evolution :: Natural selection explains apparent design without a designer.
Criticisms of the cosmological argument :: Hume: we can't infer causes beyond experience. Russell: the universe is 'just there, and that's all'. Possible infinite regress.
Teleological vs cosmological :: Teleological arguments start from order and purpose; cosmological arguments start from existence and causation.
Aquinas' third way :: Contingency: everything that exists depends on something else, so there must be a necessary being.
Copleston vs Russell (1948) :: Copleston argued from contingency for a necessary being; Russell rejected the need for an explanation of the universe.
Anthropic principle :: Tennant: the universe is fine-tuned for life, suggesting design.
# ontological | Philosophy | Arguments based on reason
Anselm's ontological argument :: God is 'that than which nothing greater can be conceived'; existing in reality is greater than existing only in the mind, so God must exist.
Gaunilo's island :: Anselm's logic would prove the existence of a perfect island, which is absurd.
Kant's criticism :: Existence is not a predicate: it adds nothing to the concept of a thing.
Descartes' ontological argument :: God is a supremely perfect being; existence is a perfection, so God exists, just as a triangle must have three angles.
A priori vs a posteriori :: Ontological arguments are a priori (reason alone); design and cosmological arguments are a posteriori (experience).
Anselm's second form :: God's existence is necessary; a being that cannot not exist is greater than one that can.
Anselm's reply to Gaunilo :: The argument only applies to God, a necessary being, not to contingent things like islands.
# experience | Philosophy | Religious experience
Types of religious experience :: Visions, numinous experiences (Otto: mysterium tremendum et fascinans), mystical experiences, conversion and corporate experiences (e.g. the Toronto Blessing).
William James :: Mystical experiences are ineffable, noetic, transient and passive; judged by their fruits.
Swinburne's principles :: Principle of credulity (trust experiences unless there's reason not to) and principle of testimony (trust what others report).
Challenges to religious experience :: Explained by psychology (Freud: wish fulfilment), physiology (temporal lobe) or drugs; experiences are subjective and unverifiable.
Corporate religious experience :: Shared experiences, e.g. the Toronto Blessing (1994) with laughter and falling; criticised as mass hysteria.
Conversion :: A change of belief or way of life, e.g. St Paul on the road to Damascus.
Otto's numinous :: A feeling of awe and wonder before the holy, a 'wholly other' presence.
Value of religious experience :: May be self-authenticating for the believer, but less convincing as proof for others.
# evil | Philosophy | The problem of evil
Logical problem of evil :: Mackie: God is omnipotent and wholly good, yet evil exists; this is inconsistent.
Evidential problem of evil :: Rowe: the amount of pointless suffering (e.g. a fawn dying in a forest fire) makes God's existence unlikely.
Augustinian theodicy :: Evil is a privation of good; it came from the free will of angels and humans (the Fall); natural evil is a punishment. Soul-deciding.
Irenaean theodicy (Hick) :: Humans are created in the image of God and must grow into his likeness through a 'vale of soul-making'; epistemic distance allows free choice.
Criticisms of theodicies :: Augustine conflicts with evolution and is unjust to later generations; Hick's view struggles with the scale of suffering (e.g. the Holocaust) and universal salvation.
Natural and moral evil :: Moral evil is caused by human choices; natural evil comes from the natural world (earthquakes, disease).
The free will defence :: God allows evil because a world with free agents is better than one with robots.
Epistemic distance :: Hick: God keeps a distance so humans can freely choose to love him.
Dostoevsky's challenge :: Ivan Karamazov rejects any heaven built on the suffering of innocent children.
# god-attributes | Philosophy | The nature of God and religious language
Omnipotence :: God can do anything logically possible (Aquinas) or can do anything at all (Descartes).
Eternity :: Boethius: God is timeless and sees all of time at once. Swinburne: God is everlasting and moves through time.
Omniscience and free will :: If God knows our future actions, are we free? Boethius: God sees our actions as an eternal present, so foreknowledge doesn't cause them.
Apophatic and cataphatic language :: Apophatic: the via negativa describes God by what he is not (Pseudo-Dionysius). Cataphatic: positive statements through analogy and symbol.
Analogy :: Aquinas: analogy of attribution and proportion allow meaningful talk about God.
Symbol (Tillich) :: Religious language points to and participates in what it symbolises: God is the 'ground of being'.
Logical positivism :: Ayer's verification principle: statements are meaningful only if analytic or empirically verifiable; religious statements are meaningless.
Falsification :: Flew: religious claims die 'the death of a thousand qualifications'. Hare's bliks and Mitchell's partisan respond.
Wittgenstein's language games :: Meaning comes from use within a form of life; religious language is meaningful to believers.
Omnibenevolence :: God is perfectly good; questioned by the problem of evil and divine commands in scripture.
Euthyphro dilemma :: Is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good?
The stone paradox :: Can God create a stone he cannot lift? A challenge to the coherence of omnipotence.
Via negativa criticism :: Saying only what God is not may leave nothing meaningful to believe in.
Hare's blik :: A non-falsifiable way of seeing the world, like the student convinced all dons want to kill him.
Mitchell's partisan :: Faith in the stranger despite ambiguous evidence; believers allow evidence to count against belief but not decisively.
# natural-law | Ethics | Natural law
Aquinas' natural law :: Humans have a God-given telos; reason discovers primary precepts: preserve life, reproduce, educate, live in society, worship God.
Secondary precepts :: Specific rules derived from the primary precepts, e.g. no abortion follows from preserving life.
Doctrine of double effect :: An action with a good and a bad effect is permissible if the act is good, the bad effect isn't intended, it is not the means to the good, and there is proportionate reason.
Criticisms of natural law :: Assumes a single human nature; the naturalistic fallacy; inflexible in complex situations.
Four tiers of law :: Eternal law, divine law, natural law and human law.
Real and apparent goods :: Humans may pursue apparent goods by mistake, but reason helps identify real goods.
Interior and exterior acts :: Aquinas: both the intention and the act must be good.
Strengths of natural law :: Universal, based on reason, gives clear rules and is accessible to non-religious people.
# situation | Ethics | Situation ethics
Fletcher's situation ethics :: The only intrinsic good is agape (selfless love); act in the most loving way in each situation.
Four working principles :: Pragmatism, relativism, positivism and personalism.
Six fundamental principles :: Love is the only good; love is the ruling norm; love and justice are the same; love wills the neighbour's good; only the end justifies the means; love decides there and then.
Criticisms of situation ethics :: Hard to predict consequences; too individualistic; agape is subjective; William Temple supported it, but the Pope condemned it in 1952.
Fletcher's three approaches :: Legalism (rigid rules), antinomianism (no rules) and situationism (love guides each case).
Agape in the Bible :: Jesus' command to love your neighbour and the Good Samaritan underpin situation ethics.
Fletcher's examples :: Mrs Bergmeier, who became pregnant to be released from a prison camp to her family.
Strengths of situation ethics :: Flexible, person-centred and based on Christian love.
# kant | Ethics | Kantian ethics
Duty and the good will :: Kant: the only thing good without qualification is a good will acting from duty.
Hypothetical and categorical imperatives :: Hypothetical: if you want X, do Y. Categorical: unconditional moral commands.
Formulations of the categorical imperative :: Universal law (act only on maxims you can will to be universal), persons as ends (never only as means) and the kingdom of ends.
Postulates :: Freedom, immortality and God are assumed for morality to make sense (the summum bonum).
Criticisms of Kant :: Conflicts of duties; ignores consequences (the murderer at the door); too rigid; ignores emotions.
Kant's good will :: The motive, not the consequences, determines moral worth: acting from duty, not merely in accordance with it.
The shopkeeper example :: A shopkeeper who is honest for profit acts in accordance with duty but not from duty.
Autonomy :: Kant: moral agents give themselves the moral law through reason, rather than following authority.
Strengths of Kant :: Universal, respects human dignity and gives clear rules that don't depend on predicting outcomes.
# util | Ethics | Utilitarianism
Bentham's act utilitarianism :: The right act produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number; measured with the hedonic calculus (intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, extent).
Mill's rule utilitarianism :: Higher (intellectual) and lower (bodily) pleasures; follow rules that generally maximise happiness; the harm principle.
Criticisms of utilitarianism :: Ignores justice and rights (tyranny of the majority); consequences are hard to predict; happiness is hard to measure.
Principle of utility :: Actions are right if they promote happiness and wrong if they produce the reverse.
Mill's quote :: 'It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied.'
Act vs rule utilitarianism :: Act judges each situation; rule follows rules that generally produce the greatest happiness.
Strengths of utilitarianism :: Simple, democratic, considers consequences and treats each person's happiness equally.
# applied | Ethics | Euthanasia and business ethics
Euthanasia terms :: Voluntary, non-voluntary, active and passive euthanasia. Sanctity of life vs quality of life and autonomy.
Euthanasia and the theories :: Natural law opposes it (preserving life); situation ethics may support it if loving; Kant: autonomy vs duty to preserve life; utilitarianism weighs suffering.
Business ethics :: Corporate social responsibility, whistleblowing, good ethics as good business, and globalisation.
Business ethics and the theories :: Kant: never treat employees or customers only as means. Utilitarianism: maximise wellbeing of stakeholders.
Sanctity of life :: Life is sacred and God-given (Genesis 1:27); only God may take it.
Quality of life :: Peter Singer: what matters is the quality of life and the person's interests, not life itself.
The Netherlands and Switzerland :: Examples of countries where forms of euthanasia or assisted dying are legal.
Whistleblowing :: Exposing wrongdoing in a company; Kant may support it as a duty to truth, while it may harm stakeholders.
# metaethics | Ethics | Meta-ethics and conscience
Naturalism :: Moral terms describe natural facts and can be verified, e.g. utilitarianism.
Intuitionism :: Moore: 'good' is indefinable but recognised by intuition; the naturalistic fallacy and the open question argument.
Emotivism :: Ayer: moral statements express emotions ('boo/hurrah' theory) and are neither true nor false.
Aquinas on conscience :: Synderesis (innate knowledge of good) and conscientia (applying it with reason).
Freud on conscience :: Conscience is the super-ego, formed from parental and social rules, causing guilt.
Sexual ethics :: Debates on premarital and extramarital sex and homosexuality, applying natural law, situation ethics, Kant and utilitarianism.
Cognitivism and non-cognitivism :: Cognitivist theories say moral statements can be true or false; non-cognitivist theories say they cannot.
Prescriptivism :: Hare: moral statements are prescriptions that should be universalisable.
Butler on conscience :: Conscience is a God-given faculty of reflection that guides self-love and benevolence.
Fromm on conscience :: Authoritarian conscience (internalised authority) vs humanistic conscience (our own true self).
# christian | Christian thought | Developments in Christian thought
Augustine on human nature :: Before the Fall humans lived in harmony; original sin brought concupiscence. Grace is needed for salvation.
Death and the afterlife :: Heaven, hell and purgatory; the parable of the sheep and the goats; election debates (limited vs universal salvation).
Knowledge of God :: Natural knowledge (reason and creation, sensus divinitatis - Calvin) vs revealed knowledge (scripture, Jesus Christ).
The person of Jesus :: Son of God, teacher of wisdom and liberator; examples include his miracles, the Sermon on the Mount and challenging authorities.
Christian moral principles :: Theonomous (Bible alone), heteronomous (Bible, church and reason) and autonomous (love) approaches.
Bonhoeffer :: Christian duty to resist tyranny; costly vs cheap grace; religionless Christianity; involvement in the plot against Hitler.
Religious pluralism :: Exclusivism, inclusivism (Rahner's anonymous Christians) and pluralism (Hick).
Gender and theology :: Ruether: God as female; Daly: 'if God is male, then the male is God'.
Secularism :: Freud and Dawkins saw religion as harmful; debates about faith schools and the role of Christianity in public life.
Liberation theology :: Arose in Latin America; preferential option for the poor; uses Marxist analysis of structural sin (Gutiérrez, Boff).
Augustine on sex :: Concupiscence made sexual desire uncontrolled after the Fall.
Calvin's predestination :: God chooses the elect for salvation in advance (double predestination).
Purgatory :: A Catholic belief in a state of purification before heaven; rejected by most Protestants.
Hick's pluralism :: Different religions are paths to the same Real; the Copernican revolution in theology.
Bonhoeffer's Cost of Discipleship :: Cheap grace is forgiveness without repentance; costly grace requires following Jesus.
Christian feminism :: Ruether argued that Jesus challenged patriarchy; the Church has been shaped by male power.
Marx and liberation theology :: Liberation theology uses Marx's analysis of exploitation but rejects his atheism.
Dawkins on religion :: Religion is a delusion that causes harm; faith schools indoctrinate children.
