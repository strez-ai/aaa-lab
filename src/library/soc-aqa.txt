# edu-role | 4.1 | Education: role and functions
Durkheim on education :: Education creates social solidarity by passing on shared norms and values (e.g. through history lessons), and teaches specialist skills needed for a complex division of labour. School is a 'society in miniature'.
Parsons on education :: School is the focal socialising agency linking family and society. It moves children from particularistic standards to universalistic ones and is meritocratic, so status is achieved, not ascribed.
Davis and Moore :: Education performs role allocation: it sifts and sorts people, so the most able get the most important, highest-rewarded jobs.
Human capital theory :: Schultz: spending on education is an investment that makes the workforce more productive and benefits the economy.
Althusser :: Education is an ideological state apparatus that reproduces class inequality and legitimates it, making inequality seem fair.
Bowles and Gintis :: The correspondence principle: school mirrors the workplace, creating a docile workforce through the hidden curriculum. Meritocracy is a myth.
Willis, Learning to Labour :: The 'lads' formed a counter-school culture that rejected school and prepared them for manual work. Challenges the idea that pupils are passively shaped.
New Right on education :: Chubb and Moe: state education fails because it lacks competition; marketisation and parental choice raise standards. Education should teach a shared national culture.
Criticisms of functionalism on education :: Ignores inequalities of class, gender and ethnicity; Marxists say education transmits ruling-class ideology; interactionists say it ignores pupils' agency.
Neoliberalism and education :: Education should serve the economy by producing skilled workers; schools should compete in a market.
Postmodernism and education :: Education is becoming more diverse and personalised, reflecting a fragmented society (Usher and Edwards).
# edu-class | 4.1 | Education: social class and achievement
Cultural deprivation :: Working-class children are said to lack the language, attitudes and values needed for success. Douglas: less parental interest. Feinstein: parents' education matters. Criticised for victim-blaming (Keddie).
Bernstein: speech codes :: Working class use the restricted code (context-bound, short); middle class use the elaborated code, which matches school, giving them an advantage.
Material deprivation :: Poverty affects achievement through poor diet, housing, cost of uniforms and trips (Flaherty), and needing to work part-time. Ridge: children in poverty miss out on activities.
Bourdieu: cultural capital :: Middle-class pupils have cultural capital (knowledge, attitudes, tastes) valued by schools, plus economic and educational capital, which reproduces class advantage.
Gewirtz: parental choice :: Privileged-skilled choosers use cultural capital to get children into better schools; disconnected-local choosers lack it.
Labelling and teachers :: Becker: teachers judge pupils against the 'ideal pupil', usually middle class. Rist: pupils placed on tables by perceived ability. Labels can lead to a self-fulfilling prophecy (Rosenthal and Jacobson).
Streaming and the A-to-C economy :: Gillborn and Youdell: schools ration attention to pupils near the C/4 borderline, so working-class pupils in lower sets are neglected.
Pupil subcultures :: Lacey: differentiation and polarisation create pro- and anti-school subcultures. Ball: banding reduced but didn't remove inequalities.
Archer: identity and class :: Working-class pupils may reject education as 'not for the likes of us' and gain symbolic capital through 'Nike identities'.
Ethos of a school :: The culture and expectations of a school, which may favour middle-class pupils.
Ball and Reay: working-class university choices :: Working-class students often choose local universities to reduce costs and feel less out of place.
# edu-gender | 4.1 | Education: gender
Why girls outperform boys :: External: impact of feminism, changes in family and women's employment, changing ambitions (Sharpe). Internal: equal opportunities policies, coursework, positive role models, fewer barriers.
Boys' underachievement :: Poorer literacy, decline of traditional male jobs ('crisis of masculinity', Mitsos and Browne), feminisation of education, laddish subcultures (Epstein).
Gender and subject choice :: Gendered early socialisation, gendered subject images (Kelly: science as a boys' subject), peer pressure and gendered career opportunities shape choices.
Gender identity and school :: Verbal and physical abuse such as homophobic language polices masculinity (Connell). Girls face 'slag' labels (Lees).
Archer: working-class girls :: Hyper-heterosexual feminine identities gain symbolic capital but conflict with school, so some girls underachieve.
Gender gap in A levels :: Girls achieve more top grades in most subjects, but boys are still more likely to take physics and computing.
# edu-ethnicity | 4.1 | Education: ethnicity
Ethnic differences in achievement :: Chinese and Indian pupils achieve above average; Black Caribbean and Gypsy Roma pupils below. Varies with class and gender.
External factors and ethnicity :: Cultural deprivation (language, family structure, attitudes - Sewell's view on lack of fatherly nurturing), material deprivation and racism in wider society.
Labelling and racism in schools :: Gillborn and Youdell: teachers expect Black pupils to be badly behaved, leading to conflict and exclusions. Wright: Asian pupils marginalised.
Pupil responses :: Fuller: Black girls rejected labels but worked hard. Mac an Ghaill: responses vary. Sewell: rebels, conformists, retreatists and innovators among Black boys.
Institutional racism :: Gillborn: 'locked-in' inequality through ethnocentric curriculum, assessment (e.g. Foundation Stage Profile) and access to opportunities such as gifted and talented schemes.
Ethnocentric curriculum :: A curriculum that reflects the culture of one ethnic group, e.g. 'little Englandism' in history (Ball).
Critical race theory :: Racism is embedded in institutions, not just individual attitudes; locked-in inequality.
# edu-policy | 4.1 | Education: policy
1988 Education Reform Act :: Conservative marketisation: league tables, Ofsted, formula funding, national curriculum and open enrolment, creating a 'parentocracy' (David).
Ball and Whitty on marketisation :: Marketisation reproduces inequality: popular schools can be selective (cream-skimming and silt-shifting, Bartlett).
New Labour policies :: Reducing inequality through Education Action Zones, Aim Higher, EMA and academies, while continuing marketisation.
Coalition policies :: Academies and free schools; the pupil premium for disadvantaged pupils; criticised as fragmentation and centralisation of control.
Privatisation of education :: Ball: private companies increasingly run services and sell to schools (e.g. exam boards, academy chains), turning education into a commodity.
Globalisation and education :: Education policy is shaped by international comparisons such as PISA league tables, and by the need for a skilled workforce to compete globally.
# methods | 4.1 | Research methods
Positivism vs interpretivism :: Positivists prefer quantitative, reliable, scientific methods to find social facts. Interpretivists prefer qualitative methods that are valid and understand meanings.
PET factors :: Practical (time, cost, access), ethical (consent, harm, confidentiality, deception) and theoretical (reliability, validity, representativeness) issues in choosing methods.
Questionnaires :: Quick, cheap, reliable, good for large samples and correlations. But low validity, imposition problem, and low response rates.
Structured and unstructured interviews :: Structured interviews are reliable and comparable. Unstructured interviews have high validity and rapport but are time-consuming and open to interviewer bias.
Participant observation :: Covert or overt observation of a group. High validity and insight (e.g. Venkatesh), but risk of 'going native', the Hawthorne effect (overt) and ethical issues (covert).
Experiments :: Lab experiments are rarely used due to artificiality and ethics. Field experiments, e.g. Rosenthal and Jacobson, happen in natural settings.
Official statistics :: Cheap, large-scale and allow trends to be compared, but may be socially constructed and reflect how they were collected.
Documents :: Public, personal and historical documents. Scott's criteria: authenticity, credibility, representativeness and meaning.
Reliability, validity and representativeness :: Reliable: same results when repeated. Valid: a true picture. Representative: the sample reflects the population so findings can be generalised.
Sampling :: Random, systematic, stratified, quota, snowball and opportunity sampling. Sampling frames may be unavailable for some groups.
Group interviews and focus groups :: Interviewing several people together; can generate ideas but may be dominated by some members.
Longitudinal studies :: Following a group over time to identify change; valuable but costly and suffer from sample attrition.
Ethics: BSA guidelines :: Informed consent, confidentiality, avoid harm, avoid deception, and legality.
# mic | 4.1 | Methods in context (education)
Researching pupils :: Pupils are less powerful, may be less articulate and are vulnerable, so consent from parents and schools is needed.
Researching teachers :: Teachers are busy and may act differently when observed (impression management) to look good for inspection.
Researching schools :: Gatekeepers (headteachers) control access; legal and ethical rules limit research; school data such as exam results are easily available.
Researching parents :: Middle-class parents are more likely to take part; parents may give socially desirable answers about their parenting.
Classroom observation :: Useful for studying labelling and interaction, but teachers and pupils may change behaviour; covert observation is hard in schools.
# theory | 4.2 | Sociological theory
Functionalism :: A consensus theory: society is like an organism with interdependent parts. Value consensus creates social order. Key thinkers: Durkheim, Parsons, Merton.
Merton's criticisms of Parsons :: Indispensability, functional unity and universal functionalism are assumptions; he introduced manifest and latent functions and dysfunctions.
Marxism :: Conflict between the bourgeoisie and proletariat. The economic base shapes the superstructure; ideology hides exploitation. Humanistic and structuralist Marxism differ on agency.
Feminism :: Liberal (equal rights through reform), radical (patriarchy is universal, separatism), Marxist (women's oppression benefits capitalism), and difference feminism (women aren't one group).
Social action theories :: Weber: understand meaning (verstehen). Symbolic interactionism (Mead, Blumer), labelling, phenomenology and ethnomethodology focus on how individuals create society.
Structuration :: Giddens: structure and agency are two sides of the same coin; people reproduce structures through their actions but can also change them (reflexivity).
Postmodernism :: Lyotard: rejection of metanarratives. Baudrillard: hyperreality and simulacra. Society is fragmented and identities are chosen.
Late modernity :: Giddens and Beck argue we're in late modernity, not postmodernity: rapid change, globalisation, reflexivity and risk society, but rational action can still improve society.
Consensus vs conflict theories :: Consensus theories (functionalism) stress shared values; conflict theories (Marxism, feminism) stress inequality and power.
Structural vs action theories :: Structural theories see behaviour shaped by society; action theories see individuals constructing society.
# sci | 4.2 | Science, values and social policy
Is sociology a science? :: Positivists say yes, using quantitative methods. Popper: science is based on falsification. Kuhn: science works within paradigms; sociology is pre-paradigmatic. Realists (Sayer) say both study unobservable structures.
Value freedom :: Weber: values should guide choice of topic but not data collection. Positivists aim for value freedom; Gouldner and Becker argue it's impossible and undesirable.
Sociology and social policy :: Positivists and functionalists see a direct role. Marxists see policies serving capitalism. Feminists influenced equality laws. The New Right supported reduced state intervention (Murray).
# fam-couples | 4.3 | Families: couples and domestic labour
Domestic division of labour :: Parsons: instrumental (male breadwinner) and expressive (female homemaker) roles. Young and Willmott: the symmetrical family.
Feminist views on housework :: Oakley: housewife role is oppressive; husband 'helps' rather than shares. Gershuny: lagged adaptation. Ferri and Smith: dual burden.
Resources and decision making :: Pahl and Vogler: pooling vs allowance systems; men usually make major financial decisions.
Emotion work and triple shift :: Duncombe and Marsden: women do the dual burden plus emotion work, a triple shift.
Domestic violence :: Dobash and Dobash: violence reflects patriarchy. Wilkinson: linked to stress from inequality. Radical feminists see it as a product of patriarchy; official statistics underestimate it.
Gender scripts :: Dunne: lesbian couples share household tasks more equally because they lack gender scripts.
Silver and Schor: commercialisation of housework :: Housework is reduced through buying goods and services, though feminists say it remains women's responsibility.
# fam-child | 4.3 | Families: childhood
Childhood as socially constructed :: Ariès: in the Middle Ages there was no separate idea of childhood. Pilcher: modern childhood is a separate life stage. Varies across cultures (Punch, Holmes).
March of progress view :: Childhood is better than ever: lower infant mortality, child-centred families, legal protection (Aries, Shorter).
Conflict view of childhood :: Inequalities between children (class, gender, ethnicity) and between adults and children; adult control over children's space, time and bodies (Hockey and James).
Disappearance of childhood :: Postman: TV and information hierarchy blur adult and child. Palmer: toxic childhood from technology and consumerism.
Child-centredness :: The family revolves around children's needs, partly due to smaller families and rights such as the Children Act 1989.
Children's rights :: UN Convention on the Rights of the Child; children's legal protection has increased but they remain dependent.
# fam-demog | 4.3 | Families: demography
Falling birth rate :: Changes in women's position, decline in infant mortality, children as an economic liability and child-centredness.
Falling death rate and ageing population :: Improved nutrition, medicine, public health and living standards. An ageing population increases the dependency ratio and creates 'ageism'.
Migration :: Net migration affects population size and age structure. Globalisation increases diversity, transnational identities and feminisation of migration.
Life expectancy :: The average number of years a person can expect to live; increased due to medicine and better living conditions.
Beanpole family :: Brannen: families with few siblings but more generations alive, due to longer life and lower birth rates.
# fam-diversity | 4.3 | Families: diversity and change
Changing patterns :: Fewer marriages, more divorce, cohabitation, lone-parent families, same-sex couples, living apart together and singlehood.
Reasons for rising divorce :: Changes in law (1969 Divorce Reform Act), declining stigma, secularisation, higher expectations of marriage (Fletcher) and women's financial independence.
Rapoports: five types of diversity :: Organisational, cultural, class, life-stage and generational diversity.
Postmodernist view of family :: Stacey: women created the 'divorce-extended family'. Beck: negotiated families in a risk society. Giddens: pure relationships based on choice.
Personal life perspective :: Smart: we should study all relationships people see as important, including friends, pets and dead relatives.
Lone-parent families :: Over 90% are headed by women; increase linked to divorce and births outside marriage. The New Right sees them as dysfunctional.
Same-sex families :: Weeks: chosen families. Legal changes such as civil partnerships (2004) and same-sex marriage (2014) have increased their acceptance.
# fam-policy | 4.3 | Families: theories and social policy
Functionalist view of the family :: Murdock: four functions (sexual, reproductive, economic, educational). Parsons: primary socialisation and stabilisation of adult personalities.
Marxist view of the family :: Engels: family passes on private property. Zaretsky: a haven from capitalism. Family is a unit of consumption and socialises children into capitalist ideology.
Feminist views of the family :: Liberal feminists see gradual progress. Radical feminists see the family as a patriarchal institution. Marxist feminists see it serving capitalism (Ansley: women as 'takers of shit').
Family policies :: Examples: maternity and paternity leave, child benefit, same-sex marriage (2014), welfare reforms. Donzelot: policy as state surveillance. New Right sees welfare as undermining the family (Murray).
Social policy and family diversity :: Feminists argue policies assume the conventional family; Leonard: policies reinforce women's roles.
# bel-theories | 4.5 | Beliefs: theories of religion
Functionalist views on religion :: Durkheim: the sacred and profane; worship of totems is worship of society; creates the collective conscience. Malinowski: religion helps cope with uncertainty. Parsons: legitimises core values.
Marxist views on religion :: Religion is 'the opium of the people', an ideology that legitimates suffering and inequality and creates false consciousness.
Feminist views on religion :: Religion is patriarchal: male-dominated organisations, sacred texts and exclusion of women from roles. Woodhead argues religion can be used to gain freedom.
Religion as a force for change :: Weber: Calvinism's Protestant ethic contributed to capitalism. Liberation theology (Maduro), Bruce on the US civil rights movement.
Neo-Marxism and religion :: Gramsci and Maduro: religion can be relatively autonomous and challenge the ruling class.
Postmodern views on religion :: Lyon: Jesus in Disneyland; religion has relocated to consumption and the media.
# bel-orgs | 4.5 | Beliefs: religious organisations
Church and sect :: Troeltsch: churches are large, bureaucratic and universalistic; sects are small, exclusive, often led by a charismatic leader.
Denominations and cults :: Niebuhr: denominations are less exclusive than sects. Cults are loose, individualistic and offer specific services.
New religious movements :: Wallis: world-rejecting, world-accommodating and world-affirming.
Growth of sects and NRMs :: Marginality, relative deprivation and social change (Wilson) explain their appeal. Stark and Bainbridge: cycle of renewal.
New Age movements :: Heelas: emphasise self-spirituality and detraditionalisation; appeal in late modernity.
Gender, ethnicity and age and religiosity :: Women are more religious than men (Miller and Hoffman). Ethnic minorities use religion for cultural defence and transition (Bruce). Older people are more religious.
Religious market theory :: Stark and Bainbridge: religions compete like companies; diversity increases participation.
# bel-change | 4.5 | Beliefs: secularisation
Secularisation :: Wilson: the process by which religious thinking, practice and institutions lose social significance.
Evidence for secularisation in the UK :: Declining church attendance, fewer church weddings and baptisms, decline of religious influence on law and politics.
Explanations of secularisation :: Rationalisation (Weber: disenchantment), structural differentiation (Parsons), social and cultural diversity (Bruce), religious diversity.
Against secularisation :: Davie: believing without belonging and vicarious religion. Spiritual revolution (Heelas and Woodhead). Religious market theory (Stark and Bainbridge). Global growth of religion.
Religion in a global context :: Fundamentalism as a response to globalisation (Giddens). Huntington: clash of civilisations. Growth of Pentecostalism in Latin America.
Secularisation in the USA :: Bruce: secularisation from within, where religion becomes more like a psychological therapy.
# media-own | 4.6 | Media: ownership and control
Concentration of ownership :: A few large conglomerates own much of the media, e.g. Murdoch's News Corp. Bagdikian: a handful of corporations control the global media.
Pluralist view of the media :: Media reflect consumer demand; competition and diversity give people choice; journalists are independent.
Marxist view of the media :: Instrumentalist Marxists: owners directly control content. Hegemonic (neo-Marxists, Glasgow University Media Group): journalists share middle-class values and produce consensus.
News values and gatekeeping :: Galtung and Ruge: events are selected by news values (e.g. negativity, personalisation, elite persons). Gatekeepers and agenda-setting shape what the audience sees.
Postmodern view of the media :: Media saturation blurs reality and representation; audiences choose identities from media images.
# media-new | 4.6 | Media: new media
New media features :: Digital, interactive, hypertextual and networked. Users create content.
Optimistic views of new media :: Cultural optimists: democratise information, give voice to the powerless (e.g. the Arab Spring), create participation.
Pessimistic views of new media :: Cultural pessimists: surveillance, digital divide, corporate domination (Google, Meta), and misinformation.
# media-rep | 4.6 | Media: representations
Representations of gender :: Tuchman: symbolic annihilation of women. Mulvey: male gaze. Representations are changing but stereotypes persist.
Representations of ethnicity :: Ethnic minorities are under-represented or portrayed negatively (Van Dijk: as a problem). Alvarado: humorous, dangerous, pitied and exotic stereotypes.
Representations of class and age :: Working class shown as problematic (e.g. chav culture); the young as a threat (moral panics) and the old as frail or invisible.
Representations of disability and sexuality :: Disabled people under-represented and stereotyped (Barnes). LGBTQ+ people historically shown through stereotypes, though representation is increasing.
Moral panics :: Cohen: exaggerated media reaction to a group seen as a threat (folk devils), leading to calls for control.
# media-effects | 4.6 | Media: effects
Hypodermic syringe model :: The audience is passive and directly influenced by media messages, e.g. Bandura's Bobo doll experiments.
Two-step flow model :: Katz and Lazarsfeld: opinion leaders interpret media messages for others.
Uses and gratifications :: The audience is active and uses media to meet needs such as diversion, personal relationships, personal identity and surveillance (Blumler and McQuail).
Cultivation theory :: Gerbner: heavy exposure over time cultivates a distorted view of reality.
Reception analysis :: Hall: audiences read messages in dominant, negotiated or oppositional ways.
Desensitisation :: Repeated exposure to media violence may reduce sensitivity to real-life violence.
# crime-theories | 4.9 | Crime: theories
Functionalist view of crime :: Durkheim: crime is inevitable and functional, reinforcing boundaries and allowing change. Merton: strain between cultural goals and legitimate means leads to innovation, ritualism, retreatism and rebellion.
Subcultural theories :: Cohen: status frustration among working-class boys. Cloward and Ohlin: criminal, conflict and retreatist subcultures. Miller: lower-class focal concerns.
Labelling theory :: Becker: deviance is behaviour so labelled. Lemert: primary and secondary deviance. Cicourel: negotiation of justice. Cohen: moral panics and deviance amplification.
Marxist views of crime :: Crime is a product of capitalism; laws protect the ruling class; selective enforcement targets the working class. Chambliss: laws protect property.
Neo-Marxism: critical criminology :: Taylor, Walton and Young: crime is a conscious choice with political motives; a fully social theory of deviance is needed.
Right realism :: Wilson and Kelling's broken windows; rational choice theory (Clarke); situational crime prevention and zero tolerance.
Left realism :: Lea and Young: relative deprivation, marginalisation and subculture cause crime. Solutions: improve policing, community involvement and tackle structural inequality.
Interactionist view of statistics :: Official crime statistics are socially constructed and reflect police and court decisions rather than actual crime.
Hirschi's control theory :: People conform because of attachment, commitment, involvement and belief.
# crime-patterns | 4.9 | Crime: class, gender and ethnicity
Gender and crime :: Women commit fewer crimes. Chivalry thesis (Pollak) vs bias against women (Heidensohn: double deviance). Heidensohn: control at home, school and in public limits women's crime.
Masculinity and crime :: Messerschmidt: men use crime to 'accomplish' masculinity (hegemonic and subordinated).
Ethnicity and crime :: Black people are over-represented in stop and search and prison. Left realists: relative deprivation. Neo-Marxists (Gilroy, Hall et al.): crime is constructed through racist policing and moral panics.
Class and crime :: Official statistics show working-class people commit more crime, but white-collar and corporate crime are under-recorded.
Corporate crime :: Crimes by or for companies, e.g. tax evasion, safety breaches and environmental crimes. Under-policed and under-recorded (Tombs).
Gender and victimisation :: Women are more likely to be victims of domestic and sexual violence; men of violent crime in public.
# crime-global | 4.9 | Crime: globalisation, green and state crime
Globalisation and crime :: Transnational organised crime: drugs, trafficking and cybercrime. Castells: the global criminal economy. Hobbs and Dunningham: glocal crime.
Green crime :: White: crime against the environment. Primary (direct) and secondary (breaking rules) green crime. Green criminology studies harm not just law-breaking.
State crime :: Green and Ward: illegal or deviant acts by the state. Cohen: denial of state crimes. Examples include genocide and torture.
Media and crime :: Media over-report violent and sexual crimes and create moral panics (Cohen: mods and rockers), and can cause fear of crime.
Cybercrime :: Crime using the internet, e.g. fraud, hacking and online harassment; hard to police across borders.
# crime-control | 4.9 | Crime: control, punishment and victims
Crime prevention :: Situational (target hardening, e.g. CCTV), environmental (broken windows, zero tolerance) and social and community prevention (Perry Pre-School Project).
Surveillance :: Foucault: shift from sovereign to disciplinary power; the panopticon makes people self-regulate. Actuarial justice and risk management.
Punishment :: Deterrence, rehabilitation, retribution and incapacitation. Durkheim: punishment reinforces shared values. Marxists: it maintains the ruling class.
Victims :: Positivist victimology (victim proneness) vs critical victimology (structural factors, state power). Some groups are more likely to be victimised, e.g. the poor and young men.
Criminal justice system :: Agencies include police, CPS, courts and prisons. Issues include institutional racism (Macpherson report) and rising prison populations.
Restorative justice :: Offenders meet victims and repair harm, aiming to reduce reoffending.
Rehabilitation :: Reforming offenders through education and therapy so they don't reoffend.
