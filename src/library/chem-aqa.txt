# atomic | 3.1.1 | Atomic structure
Relative charges and masses :: Proton +1, mass 1; neutron 0, mass 1; electron -1, mass about 1/1836.
Isotopes :: Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. Same chemical properties because they have the same electron configuration.
Time of flight mass spectrometer stages :: Ionisation (electron impact or electrospray), acceleration by an electric field, ion drift through the flight tube, then detection.
Electron impact vs electrospray ionisation :: Electron impact: high-energy electrons knock an electron off, giving X⁺. Electrospray: the sample dissolved in a solvent gains a proton, giving XH⁺; used for large molecules.
Relative atomic mass from a mass spectrum :: Ar = Σ(isotope mass × abundance) ÷ total abundance.
Electron configuration order :: Subshells fill 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p. Exceptions: chromium is [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ and copper is [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹.
First ionisation energy :: The energy needed to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1+ ions: X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻.
Factors affecting ionisation energy :: Nuclear charge, distance of the electron from the nucleus (atomic radius), and shielding by inner electrons.
Ionisation energy trend down a group :: Decreases: the outer electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded, so less attraction.
Ionisation energy trend across period 3 :: Generally increases due to greater nuclear charge with similar shielding. Drop at aluminium (3p electron is higher in energy) and at sulfur (paired electrons in a 3p orbital repel).
Successive ionisation energies :: Big jumps show an electron being removed from a new shell closer to the nucleus, which reveals the group of the element.
Shapes of s and p orbitals :: s orbitals are spherical; p orbitals are dumbbell-shaped. Each orbital holds two electrons with opposite spins.
Ions in electron configurations :: Transition metals lose 4s electrons before 3d electrons when forming ions, e.g. Fe²⁺ is [Ar]3d⁶.
Mass spectrum of molecules :: The peak with the highest m/z is the molecular ion and gives the Mr.
# amount | 3.1.2 | Amount of substance
Mole and Avogadro constant :: One mole contains 6.022 × 10²³ particles. Amount (mol) = mass ÷ Mr.
Ideal gas equation :: pV = nRT. Units: pressure in Pa, volume in m³, temperature in K, R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
Concentration calculations :: Concentration (mol dm⁻³) = amount (mol) ÷ volume (dm³). 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³.
Empirical and molecular formula :: Empirical: simplest whole-number ratio of atoms. Molecular: actual number of atoms, a multiple of the empirical formula found using Mr.
Percentage yield :: Actual yield ÷ theoretical yield × 100.
Atom economy :: Mass of desired product ÷ total mass of reactants × 100. High atom economy means less waste and is more sustainable.
Titration practical :: Rinse burette with the solution, fill, read from the bottom of the meniscus, add dropwise near the end point, and repeat until concordant titres (within 0.10 cm³).
Molar gas volume :: At room temperature and pressure, one mole of any gas occupies 24 dm³.
Water of crystallisation :: Heat a hydrated salt to constant mass; the mass lost is water. Use moles to find x in, e.g., CuSO4·xH2O.
Ionic equations :: Show only the species that react, leaving out spectator ions, e.g. H⁺ + OH⁻ → H2O.
# bonding | 3.1.3 | Bonding
Ionic bonding :: Electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions in a giant lattice. High melting points; conduct when molten or dissolved.
Covalent and dative bonds :: A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons. A dative (coordinate) bond is one where both electrons come from the same atom, e.g. NH4⁺.
Metallic bonding :: Attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons. Good conductors and malleable as layers slide.
Giant covalent structures :: Diamond (each C bonded to 4 others, very hard), graphite (layers, delocalised electrons conduct), silicon dioxide. Very high melting points.
VSEPR shapes :: Electron pairs repel to be as far apart as possible; lone pairs repel more than bonding pairs (each reduces angle by about 2.5°). Linear 180°, trigonal planar 120°, tetrahedral 109.5°, pyramidal 107°, bent 104.5°, trigonal bipyramidal, octahedral 90°.
Electronegativity :: The power of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond. Increases across a period and up a group; fluorine is the most electronegative.
Polar bonds and molecules :: A bond is polar if atoms have different electronegativities. A molecule is polar only if the dipoles don't cancel due to symmetry.
Van der Waals (induced dipole-dipole) forces :: Temporary dipoles induce dipoles in neighbouring molecules. Stronger with more electrons (larger molecules) and larger surface area.
Permanent dipole-dipole forces :: Attraction between polar molecules with permanent δ+ and δ- regions.
Hydrogen bonding :: Between a hydrogen atom bonded to N, O or F and a lone pair on an N, O or F of another molecule. Explains water's high boiling point and ice being less dense than water.
Bond length and strength :: Multiple bonds are shorter and stronger than single bonds.
Simple molecular substances :: Low melting and boiling points because only weak intermolecular forces must be overcome; don't conduct electricity.
Predicting structure from properties :: High melting point and conducts when molten: ionic. Conducts as solid: metallic or graphite. Very high melting point, no conduction: giant covalent.
# energetics | 3.1.4 | Energetics
Enthalpy change :: Heat energy change measured at constant pressure. Standard conditions: 100 kPa and a stated temperature (usually 298 K).
Standard enthalpy of combustion :: The enthalpy change when one mole of a substance is completely burned in oxygen, with all reactants and products in their standard states.
Standard enthalpy of formation :: The enthalpy change when one mole of a compound is formed from its elements in their standard states under standard conditions.
Calorimetry :: q = mcΔT, then ΔH = -q ÷ moles. Errors include heat loss, incomplete combustion and evaporation.
Hess's law :: The enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the route taken, so enthalpy cycles can calculate unknown enthalpy changes.
Mean bond enthalpy :: Energy needed to break one mole of a covalent bond in gaseous molecules, averaged over different compounds. ΔH = bonds broken - bonds made. Less accurate than formation data.
Exothermic and endothermic :: Exothermic reactions release heat (ΔH negative); endothermic reactions absorb heat (ΔH positive).
Enthalpy profile diagrams :: Show reactants, products, activation energy and ΔH. Catalysts lower the activation energy peak.
# kinetics | 3.1.5 | Kinetics
Collision theory :: Reactions happen when particles collide with energy greater than or equal to the activation energy and with the correct orientation.
Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution :: Shows the spread of molecular energies in a gas. The area under the curve equals the total number of molecules; it starts at the origin and never touches the x-axis.
Effect of temperature :: Increasing temperature shifts the curve right and flattens it, so many more molecules have energy above the activation energy; rate increases a lot.
Effect of concentration or pressure :: More particles per unit volume give more frequent successful collisions.
Catalysts :: Increase rate by providing an alternative reaction route with a lower activation energy, without being used up.
Measuring rate :: Collect gas in a syringe, measure mass loss, or monitor colour change. Rate = change in amount ÷ time.
Effect of surface area :: Smaller pieces have a larger surface area, so more frequent collisions.
# equilibria | 3.1.6 | Equilibria
Dynamic equilibrium :: In a closed system, the forward and reverse reactions occur at the same rate, so concentrations stay constant.
Le Chatelier's principle :: If a factor affecting an equilibrium changes, the position of equilibrium shifts to oppose the change.
Effect of temperature on equilibrium :: Increasing temperature shifts equilibrium in the endothermic direction.
Effect of pressure on equilibrium :: Increasing pressure shifts equilibrium towards the side with fewer gaseous molecules.
Catalysts and equilibrium :: Speed up forward and reverse reactions equally, so equilibrium is reached faster but its position doesn't change.
Kc :: For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, Kc = [C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ ÷ [A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ. Only temperature changes the value of Kc.
Industrial compromise :: e.g. Haber process uses moderate temperature (about 450 °C) and high pressure as a compromise between yield, rate and cost.
Haber process equation :: N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3, ΔH = -92 kJ mol⁻¹. Iron catalyst, around 200 atm and 450 °C.
Ethanol and methanol production equilibria :: Both are produced by reversible exothermic reactions, so conditions balance yield, rate and cost.
# redox | 3.1.7 | Oxidation, reduction and redox
OIL RIG :: Oxidation is loss of electrons; reduction is gain of electrons.
Oxidation state rules :: Uncombined elements are 0; oxygen is usually -2 (-1 in peroxides); hydrogen is +1 (-1 in metal hydrides); the sum equals the charge on the species.
Oxidising and reducing agents :: An oxidising agent accepts electrons and is reduced; a reducing agent donates electrons and is oxidised.
Balancing half-equations :: Balance atoms, add H2O to balance oxygen, H⁺ to balance hydrogen, then electrons to balance charge. Combine so electrons cancel.
Oxidation states in names :: Roman numerals show oxidation state, e.g. iron(III) chloride, sulfate(VI).
Redox in metal extraction :: Metal ores are reduced, e.g. iron oxide reduced by carbon monoxide in a blast furnace.
# thermodynamics | 3.1.8 | Thermodynamics (A-level)
Born-Haber cycle :: An enthalpy cycle using atomisation, ionisation energies, electron affinities and formation enthalpy to calculate lattice enthalpy.
Lattice enthalpy of formation :: The enthalpy change when one mole of a solid ionic compound is formed from its gaseous ions. More negative with smaller, more highly charged ions.
Perfect ionic model :: Assumes ions are perfect spheres with no covalent character. A difference between theoretical and experimental lattice enthalpy shows covalent character.
Enthalpy of solution :: The enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic solid dissolves in water to form an infinitely dilute solution. ΔH solution = ΔH lattice dissociation + Σ ΔH hydration of the ions.
Entropy :: A measure of disorder. Gases have more entropy than liquids, which have more than solids. ΔS = ΣS products - ΣS reactants.
Gibbs free energy :: ΔG = ΔH - TΔS. A reaction is feasible when ΔG ≤ 0. The temperature at which it becomes feasible: T = ΔH ÷ ΔS.
# rate-eq | 3.1.9 | Rate equations (A-level)
Rate equation :: Rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ, where m and n are orders found by experiment, not from the balanced equation.
Orders of reaction :: Zero order: rate unaffected by concentration. First order: rate doubles when concentration doubles. Second order: rate quadruples when concentration doubles.
Rate constant and temperature :: k increases with temperature. Arrhenius equation: k = Ae^(-Ea/RT); plot ln k against 1/T, gradient = -Ea/R.
Rate-determining step :: The slowest step in a mechanism. Species in the rate equation appear in or before the rate-determining step.
Initial rates and clock reactions :: Find the gradient at t = 0 on concentration-time graphs, or use clock reactions where 1/time is proportional to the initial rate.
Half-life :: For first-order reactions the half-life is constant, independent of concentration.
# kp | 3.1.10 | Equilibrium constant Kp (A-level)
Mole fraction :: Moles of a gas ÷ total moles of gas in the mixture.
Partial pressure :: Mole fraction × total pressure.
Kp :: Calculated like Kc but using partial pressures of gases. Only temperature affects Kp; changing pressure changes the position but not the value.
# electrode | 3.1.11 | Electrode potentials and cells (A-level)
Standard hydrogen electrode :: Hydrogen gas at 100 kPa bubbled over platinum in 1.00 mol dm⁻³ H⁺ at 298 K. Defined as 0.00 V.
Standard electrode potential :: The emf of a half-cell measured against the standard hydrogen electrode under standard conditions.
Electrochemical series :: More positive E° means a species is more easily reduced (a stronger oxidising agent). Cell emf = E°(right/positive) - E°(left/negative).
Conventional cell representation :: Zn | Zn²⁺ || Cu²⁺ | Cu: oxidised species next to the salt bridge, with the most reduced species on the outside.
Commercial cells :: Non-rechargeable, rechargeable (e.g. lithium-ion) and fuel cells. Hydrogen fuel cells produce only water but hydrogen is flammable and often made using fossil fuels.
# acids | 3.1.12 | Acids and bases (A-level)
Brønsted-Lowry acids and bases :: An acid is a proton donor; a base is a proton acceptor.
pH :: pH = -log[H⁺]. For strong acids [H⁺] = acid concentration (times number of protons).
Kw :: Ionic product of water: Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶ at 298 K. Used to find pH of strong bases.
Ka and weak acids :: Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] ÷ [HA]. For weak acids, assume [H⁺] = [A⁻], so [H⁺] = √(Ka × [HA]). pKa = -log Ka.
pH curves and indicators :: Choose an indicator whose colour change range lies within the vertical part of the pH curve. Phenolphthalein suits weak acid/strong base; methyl orange suits strong acid/weak base.
Half-neutralisation point :: For a weak acid titrated with strong base, at half the equivalence volume pH = pKa.
Buffer solution :: Resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added. Acidic buffer: weak acid and its salt. Basic buffer: ammonia and ammonium chloride.
How an acidic buffer works :: Added H⁺ reacts with the large reservoir of A⁻ to form HA; added OH⁻ reacts with HA (or H⁺, which HA replaces), so pH barely changes.
Diluting a strong acid :: Diluting by a factor of 10 raises the pH by 1.
Neutralisation enthalpy :: Strong acid-strong base neutralisation has a similar ΔH (about -57 kJ mol⁻¹) because the reaction is always H⁺ + OH⁻ → H2O.
# period3 | 3.2.1 | Periodicity
Trend in atomic radius across period 3 :: Decreases because nuclear charge increases while shielding stays similar, pulling electrons closer.
Melting points across period 3 :: Rise from Na to Al (stronger metallic bonding), peak at Si (giant covalent), drop for P4, S8, Cl2 and Ar (simple molecular, depending on van der Waals forces).
Period 3 elements with oxygen :: Na burns yellow, Mg white, Al, Si, P and S form oxides (P4O10, SO2). Metal oxides are ionic and basic; non-metal oxides are covalent and acidic.
Period 3 oxides with water :: Na2O gives NaOH (pH 14); MgO gives Mg(OH)2 (pH 9); Al2O3 and SiO2 are insoluble; P4O10 gives H3PO4; SO2 gives H2SO3; SO3 gives H2SO4.
Amphoteric aluminium oxide :: Reacts with both acids and bases.
# group2 | 3.2.2 | Group 2, the alkaline earth metals
Trends down group 2 :: Atomic radius increases, first ionisation energy decreases, reactivity increases and melting point generally decreases.
Reaction with water :: Mg reacts slowly with water but quickly with steam to form MgO and H2. Ca, Sr and Ba react more vigorously to form hydroxides.
Solubility trends :: Hydroxides become more soluble down the group; sulfates become less soluble down the group.
Uses of group 2 compounds :: Mg(OH)2 in indigestion remedies; Ca(OH)2 neutralises acidic soil; BaSO4 as barium meal for X-rays; CaO/CaCO3 remove SO2 from flue gases; Mg extracts titanium.
Test for sulfate ions :: Add acidified barium chloride solution. A white precipitate of barium sulfate shows sulfate ions.
Mg with steam :: Mg + H2O(g) → MgO + H2.
Test for group 2 cations :: Flame tests or adding NaOH: Mg²⁺ gives a white precipitate of Mg(OH)2.
# group7 | 3.2.3 | Group 7, the halogens
Trends down group 7 :: Electronegativity decreases; boiling point increases (more van der Waals forces); oxidising ability decreases.
Displacement reactions :: A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halide ion from solution, e.g. Cl2 + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br2.
Halide ions as reducing agents :: Reducing power increases down the group. With concentrated sulfuric acid, NaCl gives HCl; NaBr also gives SO2 and Br2; NaI also gives S and H2S and I2.
Silver nitrate test for halides :: Acidify with nitric acid then add silver nitrate. Cl⁻ white, Br⁻ cream, I⁻ yellow precipitates. AgCl dissolves in dilute ammonia; AgBr in concentrated; AgI in neither.
Chlorine and water :: Cl2 + H2O ⇌ HCl + HClO. Used to kill bacteria in water. Benefits outweigh the risks of toxic chlorine.
Chlorine with cold dilute NaOH :: Forms sodium chlorate(I), NaClO, used as bleach: Cl2 + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaClO + H2O.
Disproportionation :: A reaction where the same element is simultaneously oxidised and reduced.
Halogen colours :: Chlorine is a pale green gas, bromine a red-brown liquid and iodine a grey-black solid that forms a purple vapour.
# transition | 3.2.4-3.2.5 | Transition metals (A-level)
Transition metal :: An element that forms at least one stable ion with an incomplete d subshell. Characteristic properties: complex formation, coloured ions, variable oxidation states and catalytic activity.
Ligands and complexes :: A ligand donates a lone pair to form a coordinate bond with a metal ion. Monodentate (H2O, NH3, Cl⁻), bidentate (ethanedioate, ethane-1,2-diamine) and multidentate (EDTA⁴⁻).
Shapes of complexes :: Octahedral with small ligands (6 coordinate), tetrahedral with Cl⁻ (4), square planar for cisplatin, linear for Ag⁺ complexes such as Tollens' reagent.
Chelate effect :: Replacing monodentate ligands with multidentate ones increases the number of particles, so entropy increases and ΔG becomes negative.
Origin of colour :: Ligands split d orbitals. Electrons absorb visible light to move to higher orbitals (ΔE = hν), and the remaining frequencies are seen. Colour changes with oxidation state, ligand and coordination number.
Haem and carbon monoxide :: Haem is an iron(II) complex with a multidentate ligand. CO binds more strongly than O2, preventing oxygen transport.
Heterogeneous catalysts :: In a different phase from reactants; reaction happens on active sites on the surface. e.g. iron in the Haber process, V2O5 in the Contact process. Can be poisoned by impurities.
Homogeneous catalysts :: Same phase as reactants; work through an intermediate species, e.g. Fe²⁺ catalysing the reaction between I⁻ and S2O8²⁻.
Autocatalysis :: A product catalyses the reaction, e.g. Mn²⁺ in the reaction between MnO4⁻ and C2O4²⁻.
Redox titrations with manganate(VII) :: MnO4⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H2O. Self-indicating: end point is the first permanent pale pink.
Reactions of ions in aqueous solution :: [M(H2O)6]³⁺ ions are more acidic than 2+ ions due to greater charge density. With NaOH or NH3 they form hydroxide precipitates; Al(OH)3 is amphoteric.
Variable oxidation states with vanadium :: Zinc in acid reduces vanadium(V) (yellow) to (IV) blue, (III) green and (II) violet.
Tollens' reagent :: [Ag(NH3)2]⁺, a linear complex, used to distinguish aldehydes (silver mirror).
Cisplatin :: A square planar platinum(II) complex that binds to DNA in cancer cells, preventing replication. Side effects include kidney damage.
Ligand substitution with chloride :: Adding concentrated HCl to [Cu(H2O)6]²⁺ gives yellow [CuCl4]²⁻; the coordination number changes from 6 to 4 because Cl⁻ is larger.
# organic-intro | 3.3.1-3.3.2 | Organic basics and alkanes
Nomenclature :: Longest carbon chain gives the stem (meth-, eth-, prop-, but-...); functional group gives the suffix or prefix; number to give the lowest locants.
Structural isomers :: Same molecular formula, different structural formula: chain, position or functional group isomers.
Stereoisomers: E/Z :: Same structural formula, different spatial arrangement due to restricted rotation around C=C. Use Cahn-Ingold-Prelog priorities: Z = higher priority groups on the same side.
Fractional distillation of crude oil :: Crude oil is heated and vapour rises up a column with a temperature gradient; fractions condense at different heights depending on boiling point.
Cracking :: Thermal cracking (high temperature and pressure) gives mainly alkenes. Catalytic cracking (zeolite catalyst, slight pressure) gives branched alkanes, cycloalkanes and aromatics for motor fuels.
Combustion pollution :: Incomplete combustion gives toxic CO and carbon particulates. NOx forms in engines. SO2 causes acid rain, removed by flue gas desulfurisation. Catalytic converters remove CO, NOx and unburnt hydrocarbons.
Free radical substitution :: Alkanes react with chlorine in UV light: initiation (Cl2 → 2Cl•), propagation (Cl• + CH4 → HCl + •CH3; •CH3 + Cl2 → CH3Cl + Cl•) and termination.
Ozone depletion :: Chlorine radicals from CFCs catalyse ozone decomposition: Cl• + O3 → ClO• + O2; ClO• + O3 → 2O2 + Cl•. Chemists developed alternative chlorine-free compounds.
# halo | 3.3.3 | Halogenoalkanes
Nucleophilic substitution :: A nucleophile (OH⁻, CN⁻, NH3) attacks the δ+ carbon, and the halogen leaves as a halide ion. Rate depends on C-X bond enthalpy: C-I is weakest so fastest.
Products of nucleophilic substitution :: With aqueous OH⁻: alcohol. With ethanolic CN⁻: nitrile (adds a carbon). With excess ethanolic ammonia: primary amine.
Elimination :: OH⁻ in ethanol under reflux acts as a base, removing H⁺ and the halide to form an alkene.
Substitution vs elimination conditions :: Aqueous, warm conditions favour substitution; ethanolic, hot conditions favour elimination. Tertiary halogenoalkanes favour elimination.
Primary, secondary and tertiary halogenoalkanes :: Classified by the number of carbons attached to the carbon bonded to the halogen.
Nucleophile :: An electron pair donor that attacks an electron-deficient carbon.
# alkenes | 3.3.4 | Alkenes
Structure of alkenes :: The C=C double bond contains a π bond above and below the plane, which is a region of high electron density, so alkenes react with electrophiles.
Electrophilic addition with HBr :: The π bond attacks H; Br⁻ attacks the carbocation intermediate. Unsymmetrical alkenes give a major product via the more stable carbocation (tertiary > secondary > primary).
Test for alkenes :: Bromine water goes from orange to colourless.
Addition with sulfuric acid :: Concentrated H2SO4 adds across C=C to form an alkyl hydrogensulfate, which is hydrolysed by water to an alcohol.
Addition polymers :: Alkenes join by breaking the double bond. Poly(alkenes) are unreactive and don't biodegrade. Plasticisers make PVC flexible.
Carbocation stability :: Tertiary carbocations are most stable because alkyl groups push electron density towards the positive carbon (positive inductive effect).
Disposal of polymers :: Landfill, incineration (releases energy but toxic gases) and recycling. Poly(alkenes) are non-biodegradable.
# alcohols | 3.3.5 | Alcohols
Classifying alcohols :: Primary: OH carbon attached to one other carbon. Secondary: two. Tertiary: three.
Producing ethanol :: Fermentation of glucose by yeast (35 °C, anaerobic): C6H12O6 → 2C2H5OH + 2CO2. Or hydration of ethene with steam and phosphoric acid catalyst at 300 °C, 60-70 atm.
Biofuel and carbon neutrality :: Ethanol from fermentation is claimed carbon-neutral as CO2 released equals CO2 absorbed by crops, but fuel used in farming and transport adds emissions.
Oxidation of alcohols :: With acidified potassium dichromate (orange to green). Primary alcohols give aldehydes (distil) then carboxylic acids (reflux). Secondary give ketones. Tertiary don't oxidise.
Distinguishing aldehydes and ketones :: Tollens' reagent gives a silver mirror with aldehydes; Fehling's solution gives a brick-red precipitate. Ketones give no change.
Elimination of alcohols :: Heating with concentrated phosphoric or sulfuric acid removes water to form an alkene.
Primary alcohol to aldehyde :: Use excess alcohol and distil the aldehyde off as it forms, so it isn't oxidised further.
Reflux :: Heating with a condenser so vapours condense and return to the flask, allowing complete reaction without losing volatile substances.
# analysis | 3.3.6 | Organic analysis
Functional group tests :: Alkene: bromine water decolourised. Aldehyde: Tollens' or Fehling's. Alcohol: acidified dichromate orange to green (primary/secondary). Carboxylic acid: effervescence with sodium carbonate.
High-resolution mass spectrometry :: Gives precise Mr to several decimal places, which can identify the molecular formula.
Infrared spectroscopy :: Bonds absorb IR at specific wavenumbers. O-H (alcohol) broad 3230-3550 cm⁻¹; O-H (acid) very broad 2500-3000; C=O 1680-1750; fingerprint region below 1500 identifies the molecule.
Greenhouse effect and IR :: CO2, methane and water vapour absorb infrared radiation emitted from the Earth, contributing to global warming.
Identifying unknowns :: Combine test-tube tests, mass spectrometry and IR spectra to identify an organic compound.
# optical | 3.3.7 | Optical isomerism (A-level)
Chiral centre :: A carbon atom bonded to four different groups.
Enantiomers :: Non-superimposable mirror images. They rotate plane-polarised light in opposite directions.
Racemic mixture :: Equal amounts of each enantiomer; no overall rotation of plane-polarised light. Formed when a planar intermediate (e.g. carbonyl or carbocation) is attacked equally from both sides.
# carbonyl | 3.3.8-3.3.9 | Carbonyls, carboxylic acids and esters (A-level)
Reduction of carbonyls :: NaBH4 reduces aldehydes to primary alcohols and ketones to secondary alcohols by nucleophilic addition.
Nucleophilic addition with KCN :: CN⁻ attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon, then H⁺ adds to form a hydroxynitrile. Can give a racemic mixture. KCN is very toxic.
Carboxylic acids :: Weak acids that react with carbonates to give CO2. Form esters with alcohols using a concentrated sulfuric acid catalyst.
Uses of esters :: Solvents, plasticisers, perfumes and food flavourings.
Hydrolysis of esters :: Acid hydrolysis is reversible, giving acid and alcohol. Alkaline hydrolysis is irreversible, giving the carboxylate salt and alcohol.
Fats, soap and biodiesel :: Vegetable oils and animal fats are esters of glycerol. Hydrolysis with NaOH gives soap and glycerol. Reaction with methanol gives biodiesel (methyl esters).
Acylation :: Acyl chlorides and acid anhydrides react by nucleophilic addition-elimination with water (acid), alcohols (esters), ammonia (amides) and amines (N-substituted amides). Aspirin is made using ethanoic anhydride.
Acid anhydrides vs acyl chlorides :: Anhydrides are used industrially because they're cheaper, less corrosive and react less violently than acyl chlorides.
# aromatic | 3.3.10 | Aromatic chemistry (A-level)
Benzene structure :: A planar ring of six carbons with delocalised π electrons above and below the ring; all C-C bonds are the same length.
Stability of benzene :: Enthalpy of hydrogenation is less exothermic than predicted for three C=C bonds, showing delocalisation makes benzene more stable.
Electrophilic substitution :: Benzene's delocalised electrons attack an electrophile, forming an intermediate; loss of H⁺ restores the stable ring.
Nitration of benzene :: Concentrated nitric and sulfuric acids produce NO2⁺. Nitration is used to make explosives and to make amines for dyes.
Friedel-Crafts acylation :: Acyl chloride with an AlCl3 catalyst forms the electrophile RCO⁺, producing a phenyl ketone.
# amines | 3.3.11-3.3.12 | Amines, polymers and amino acids (A-level)
Preparing amines :: Primary aliphatic amines: halogenoalkane with excess ammonia, or reduce a nitrile (LiAlH4 or H2/Ni). Aromatic amines: reduce nitro compounds with tin and HCl.
Amines as bases :: The lone pair on nitrogen accepts a proton. Base strength: aliphatic amines > ammonia > aromatic amines, because alkyl groups push electron density on to N but the benzene ring withdraws it.
Quaternary ammonium salts :: Used as cationic surfactants in fabric softeners and hair conditioners.
Condensation polymers :: Polyesters (diol + dicarboxylic acid) and polyamides (diamine + dicarboxylic acid), e.g. Terylene, nylon-6,6 and Kevlar. Biodegradable by hydrolysis.
Amino acids :: Exist as zwitterions at the isoelectric point. Can act as acids and bases. Proteins are condensation polymers of amino acids.
Protein structure and hydrolysis :: Primary sequence, secondary (hydrogen bonds), tertiary (hydrogen bonds, ionic, disulfide). Hydrolysis with 6 mol dm⁻³ HCl breaks peptide links; identify amino acids by chromatography.
Enzymes and drug action :: Enzyme active sites are stereospecific, so drugs can be designed as inhibitors. Cisplatin binds to DNA bases, preventing replication in cancer cells.
DNA :: A polymer of nucleotides (phosphate, 2-deoxyribose and base). Complementary bases pair by hydrogen bonds: A-T and C-G.
# nmr | 3.3.13-3.3.16 | Synthesis, NMR and chromatography (A-level)
Organic synthesis :: Plan multi-step routes using known reactions. Fewer steps and safer reagents are better.
¹³C NMR :: Each peak represents a different carbon environment. Chemical shift (δ) depends on the environment; TMS is the reference at 0 ppm.
¹H NMR :: Number of peaks shows environments; integration gives the relative number of H atoms; splitting follows the n+1 rule from adjacent hydrogens.
Solvents for NMR :: Deuterated solvents (e.g. CDCl3) or CCl4 are used so the solvent doesn't give a ¹H signal.
Chromatography :: Separation by relative affinity for the stationary and mobile phases. TLC: Rf = distance by spot ÷ distance by solvent. Column chromatography and gas chromatography (retention time) also used.
Chemical shift :: Measured in ppm relative to TMS. Electronegative atoms nearby shift peaks to higher values (deshielding).
Interpreting splitting :: Singlet: no neighbouring H. Doublet: 1 neighbour. Triplet: 2. Quartet: 3.
