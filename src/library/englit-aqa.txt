# skills | All papers | Assessment objectives and essay skills
AO1 :: Articulate informed, personal and creative responses using associated concepts and terminology, with coherent, accurate writing.
AO2 :: Analyse ways in which meanings are shaped in literary texts (language, form and structure).
AO3 :: Demonstrate understanding of the significance and influence of the contexts in which texts are written and received.
AO4 :: Explore connections across literary texts.
AO5 :: Explore literary texts informed by different interpretations.
Essay structure :: Respond directly to the question's terms, build a line of argument, embed short quotations, analyse methods and integrate context and alternative readings.
Unseen poetry (Love through the ages) :: Compare two unseen poems: identify the speaker, form, imagery, tone and structural turns, and link them to ideas about love.
Using critics (AO5) :: Weigh critical views as alternative readings and test them, rather than just quoting a critic's name.
Writing about form (AO2) :: Discuss genre, verse vs prose, narrative voice, dramatic structure and how they shape meaning.
Comparison in the NEA :: The coursework compares two texts in about 2,500 words, one written before 1900.
# othello | Paper 1 | Othello (Shakespeare)
Othello plot :: Iago, passed over for promotion, manipulates Othello into believing Desdemona is unfaithful with Cassio. Othello kills Desdemona, learns the truth and kills himself.
Iago's motives :: Resentment at Cassio's promotion, suspicion that Othello slept with Emilia, and racial hatred; Coleridge called it 'motive-hunting of a motiveless malignity'.
Othello's tragic flaw :: Jealousy and insecurity as an outsider; his trust in 'honest Iago'. Iago calls jealousy 'the green-eyed monster'.
Desdemona :: Defies her father to marry Othello; loyal and honest; her passivity in Act 4 (the willow song) is debated by critics.
Race in Othello :: Othello is a Moor in Venice; Iago and Roderigo use racist language ('an old black ram'). Context: Elizabethan views of race and Venice as a trading city.
The handkerchief :: Othello's first gift to Desdemona, symbolising fidelity; Emilia gives it to Iago, who plants it on Cassio as 'proof'.
Emilia :: Iago's wife; her speech on the double standards of men and women (Act 4) and her final defiance in exposing Iago.
Othello's final speech :: 'one that loved not wisely but too well', reasserting his identity as a soldier before he kills himself.
Iago's soliloquies :: Reveal his plans to the audience, making them complicit: 'I am not what I am'.
Othello's speech in Act 1 :: His dignified account to the Senate of wooing Desdemona with stories of adventure.
Brabantio's warning :: 'She has deceived her father, and may thee', a seed Iago later uses.
Cassio :: Othello's lieutenant; loses his post after Iago gets him drunk; values his 'reputation'.
Roderigo :: Desdemona's rejected suitor, exploited by Iago for money and killed by him.
Setting: Venice and Cyprus :: Venice represents order and civilisation; Cyprus is a military outpost where chaos and passion take over.
Tragedy conventions :: A tragic hero of status, a flaw (hamartia), a reversal of fortune and catharsis for the audience.
Othello's language decline :: His eloquent verse breaks into fragmented prose in Act 4, mirroring his loss of control.
# gatsby | Paper 1 | The Great Gatsby (Fitzgerald)
Gatsby plot :: Narrated by Nick Carraway; Jay Gatsby throws parties to win back Daisy Buchanan, who is married to Tom. Myrtle is killed by Daisy driving Gatsby's car; George Wilson shoots Gatsby.
Nick as narrator :: Claims to be 'inclined to reserve all judgments', but is unreliable and increasingly admires Gatsby.
The green light :: At the end of Daisy's dock; symbolises Gatsby's longing and the American Dream: 'Gatsby believed in the green light'.
The valley of ashes :: A grey wasteland between West Egg and New York, symbolising moral and social decay, watched over by the eyes of Doctor T.J. Eckleburg.
Context: the Jazz Age :: 1920s America: prohibition, bootlegging, new money vs old money, consumerism and changing roles of women (flappers).
Daisy Buchanan :: Her voice is 'full of money'; she chooses the security of Tom's wealth; 'the best thing a girl can be in this world, a beautiful little fool'.
Ending of Gatsby :: 'So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past', suggesting the impossibility of recapturing the past.
Love in Gatsby :: Gatsby's idealised love for Daisy is bound up with wealth and status; Tom and Myrtle's affair is physical and possessive.
Tom Buchanan :: Wealthy, brutal and racist; represents old money's arrogance; breaks Myrtle's nose.
Myrtle Wilson :: Tom's lover from the valley of ashes, desperate to escape her class.
Jordan Baker :: A cynical golfer and Daisy's friend; Nick calls her 'incurably dishonest'.
Gatsby's parties :: Lavish and chaotic, showing Jazz Age excess; guests barely know their host.
Gatsby's past :: Born James Gatz in North Dakota; reinvented himself with the help of Dan Cody and the bootlegger Meyer Wolfshiem.
Gatsby's funeral :: Almost no one attends, revealing the emptiness of his social world.
The eyes of T.J. Eckleburg :: A billboard that George Wilson sees as the eyes of God, suggesting a world without moral judgement.
Structure of Gatsby :: Nine chapters; Gatsby's past is revealed gradually and non-chronologically; chapter 5 reunion at the centre.
# wuthering | Paper 1 | Wuthering Heights (Emily Brontë)
Wuthering Heights plot :: Heathcliff, an orphan, loves Catherine Earnshaw, who marries Edgar Linton. Heathcliff takes revenge on both families; the second generation (Cathy and Hareton) finally finds reconciliation.
Narrative structure :: Framed narrative: Lockwood records Nelly Dean's account, creating layers of unreliable narration.
Catherine's declaration :: 'I am Heathcliff', showing her sense that their souls are the same, despite her marrying Edgar for social status.
Setting :: The wild Heights (passion, nature) contrasted with Thrushcross Grange (civilisation, refinement).
Context: Gothic and Romantic :: Gothic elements (ghosts, violence, storms) and Romantic ideas of nature and passion; published 1847.
Heathcliff :: Outsider of uncertain origin; both a Byronic hero and a cruel avenger; his love becomes destructive obsession.
Isabella Linton :: Marries Heathcliff and is cruelly treated; escapes to the south and gives birth to Linton.
Catherine's ghost :: Appears to Lockwood at the window: 'Let me in!', opening the novel's supernatural theme.
Catherine's love for Edgar vs Heathcliff :: Edgar is like 'foliage in the woods'; Heathcliff is like 'the eternal rocks beneath'.
Hareton Earnshaw :: Degraded by Heathcliff as Hindley degraded him, yet he and Cathy are reconciled through learning to read.
Hindley :: Catherine's brother; mistreats Heathcliff after their father's death and declines into drink and gambling.
Ending of Wuthering Heights :: Heathcliff dies longing for Catherine; Lockwood wonders how anyone could imagine 'unquiet slumbers' for the dead.
# poetry | Paper 1 | AQA poetry anthology: love poetry through the ages
Range of the anthology :: Pre-1900 love poems including Shakespeare, Donne, Marvell, Byron, Keats, Christina Rossetti, Hardy and Browning, exploring desire, loss and devotion.
Sonnet 116 (Shakespeare) :: Defines true love as constant: 'Love is not love / Which alters when it alteration finds'; love is 'an ever-fixed mark'.
To His Coy Mistress (Marvell) :: Carpe diem argument: 'Had we but world enough, and time'; the speaker urges his lover not to delay because of mortality.
The Flea (Donne) :: A metaphysical conceit where a flea that has bitten both lovers becomes an argument for sexual union.
Porphyria's Lover (Browning) :: A dramatic monologue in which the speaker strangles his lover to preserve a perfect moment, showing possessive love.
Comparing poems :: Compare speakers, forms, imagery and attitudes to love, and how context shapes each poem's presentation of love.
Love's Philosophy (Shelley) :: The speaker argues that nature pairs everything, so his beloved should kiss him.
La Belle Dame sans Merci (Keats) :: A knight is enchanted and abandoned by a mysterious lady, leaving him 'alone and palely loitering'.
Remember (Christina Rossetti) :: A sonnet in which the speaker asks to be remembered but finally prefers her lover to 'forget and smile'.
The Ruined Maid (Hardy) :: A dialogue poem satirising Victorian morality: a 'ruined' woman is better off than her rural friend.
Whoso List to Hunt (Wyatt) :: A Petrarchan sonnet comparing pursuit of an unattainable woman to a deer hunt; often linked to Anne Boleyn.
Victorian love poetry context :: Victorian poems often explore duty, social expectation and the double standard for women.
# island | Paper 1 | The Island of Missing Trees (Shafak)
Island of Missing Trees: setting :: Set in Cyprus in 1974, during the conflict that divided the island, and in London in the late 2010s.
Island of Missing Trees: plot outline :: Kostas, a Greek Cypriot, and Defne, a Turkish Cypriot, fall in love in secret in 1970s Nicosia and are separated by the war. Years later they marry in London. After Defne's death, their 16-year-old daughter Ada tries to understand the family's buried past.
Island of Missing Trees: the fig tree narrator :: A fig tree (Ficus carica) narrates parts of the novel. It grew through the roof of the Happy Fig tavern, and a cutting was buried in Kostas's London garden. It gives a non-human, long view of love, memory and conflict.
Island of Missing Trees: the Happy Fig :: The tavern run by Yusuf (Turkish Cypriot) and Yiorgos (Greek Cypriot), a couple whose love also crosses the divide. It is where Kostas and Defne meet in secret.
Island of Missing Trees: forbidden love :: Love across ethnic, religious and political divides is shown as both powerful and dangerous, set against families and communities who forbid it.
Island of Missing Trees: memory and trauma :: Silence and inherited trauma pass between generations; Ada knows little of Cyprus. Defne later works to find the remains of the missing, linking love with grief and remembrance.
Island of Missing Trees: identity and migration :: Ada's search for identity as the child of migrants, and the idea of roots and uprooting, echoed in the trees.
Island of Missing Trees: context :: Published in 2021 by Elif Shafak, a Turkish-British writer. Draws on the 1974 division of Cyprus and the Committee on Missing Persons, which searched for people who disappeared in the conflict.
# wake | Paper 2 | Wake (Anna Hope)
Wake: setting :: London over five days in November 1920, as the body of the Unknown Warrior is brought back from France and buried in Westminster Abbey on 11 November.
Wake: structure :: Three women's stories are told in alternating sections, linked by short passages that follow the journey of the Unknown Warrior. Their lives are gradually revealed to be connected.
Wake: the three women :: Hettie, a young dance instructress at the Hammersmith Palais whose brother is traumatised by the war; Evelyn, who works at a pensions office and lost her fiancé; and Ada, a mother who cannot accept the loss of her son.
Wake: grief and remembrance :: The novel explores personal and national mourning, and how the ritual of the Unknown Warrior gives grieving people a focus when so many bodies were never identified.
Wake: aftermath of war :: Shell shock, disabled veterans, unemployment and broken families show that the war's damage continued long after 1918.
Wake: women after the war :: Women gained some independence through work and new freedoms such as dancing, but faced the loss of a generation of men and limited roles.
Wake: context :: Published in 2014, near the centenary of the First World War. Fits the 'WW1 and its aftermath' option.
# loverman | Paper 2 | Mr Loverman (Bernardine Evaristo)
Mr Loverman: plot outline :: Barrington Walker, a 74-year-old Antiguan-born man living in Hackney, has been married to Carmel for about 50 years while secretly in a relationship with his childhood friend Morris. He finally decides to come out and leave his marriage.
Mr Loverman: narrative voices :: Barrington's lively, comic first-person narration alternates with Carmel's sections, which are written in a poetic second person and titled as songs (e.g. 'Song of...').
Mr Loverman: sexuality and secrecy :: The novel explores a gay man hiding his identity for decades because of homophobia in his community and church, and the cost of that secrecy.
Mr Loverman: Carmel :: Carmel's sections reveal her loneliness, sacrifices and suspicions, so the reader sees the harm Barrington's secret caused her and she becomes a sympathetic character.
Mr Loverman: masculinity :: Barrington performs a confident, flamboyant masculinity; the novel challenges ideas of Caribbean masculinity and shows a gentler, more vulnerable side.
Mr Loverman: migration and community :: The Windrush-era Caribbean community in London, with its churches, families and expectations, shapes what Barrington and Carmel feel able to be.
Mr Loverman: ageing and family :: Love and change in old age; his daughters Donna and Maxine react differently to the truth.
Mr Loverman: context :: Published in 2013 by Bernardine Evaristo. Fits the 'Modern times: literature from 1945 to the present day' option.
# streetcar | Paper 2 | A Streetcar Named Desire (Williams)
Streetcar plot :: Blanche DuBois arrives at her sister Stella's home in New Orleans. Conflict with Stella's husband Stanley ends with Stanley raping Blanche and her being taken to an asylum.
Blanche :: A faded Southern belle hiding her past behind illusion: 'I don't want realism. I want magic!' She depends on 'the kindness of strangers'.
Stanley :: Represents the new, industrial America and masculine power; animalistic and dominant; Blanche calls him 'ape-like'.
Context: the Old South vs the New America :: The decline of the plantation aristocracy (Belle Reve) and the rise of working-class immigrants after WW2.
Symbolism in Streetcar :: The paper lantern (illusion), bathing (cleansing guilt), the Varsouviana polka (memory of Allan's suicide).
Stella :: Torn between her sister and her husband; she chooses Stanley and denial: 'I couldn't believe her story and go on living with Stanley'.
Mitch :: Stanley's friend who courts Blanche; rejects her after learning about her past.
Allan Grey :: Blanche's young husband who killed himself after she discovered his relationship with a man.
Light imagery :: Blanche avoids bright light to hide her age and past; Mitch tears off the lantern to see her 'in the light'.
Scene structure :: Eleven scenes, like episodes, building tension towards the rape in Scene 10.
Plastic theatre :: Williams used music, lighting and sound effects to show characters' inner states.
Blanche's arrival :: 'They told me to take a streetcar named Desire, and then transfer to one called Cemeteries'.
# handmaid | Paper 2 | The Handmaid's Tale (Atwood)
Handmaid's Tale plot :: In the theocratic Republic of Gilead, Offred is a Handmaid forced to bear children for a Commander. Her story ends ambiguously as she steps into a van.
Narrative and structure :: First-person, fragmented and retrospective; the Historical Notes reframe the story as an academic artefact from 2195.
Control of women :: Women are categorised by role (Wives, Handmaids, Marthas, Aunts); they're banned from reading; names reflect ownership (Of-fred).
Context :: Written in 1985 in response to the rise of the religious right in the US, and influenced by Orwell's 1984.
'Nolite te bastardes carborundorum' :: Mock Latin for 'don't let the bastards grind you down'; scratched in Offred's cupboard, a symbol of resistance.
Moira :: Offred's rebellious friend who escapes the Red Centre but ends up at Jezebel's, showing the limits of resistance.
The Commander :: Fred, a powerful man in Gilead who breaks rules by playing Scrabble with Offred.
Serena Joy :: The Commander's Wife, a former gospel singer who campaigned for women to stay at home.
Ofglen and Mayday :: Offred's shopping partner, part of the resistance network Mayday.
Nick :: The Commander's driver; Offred's lover, who may help her escape at the end.
The Ceremony :: The monthly ritual of the Commander attempting to impregnate Offred, justified by the biblical story of Rachel and Bilhah.
Offred's memories :: Flashbacks to her husband Luke, her daughter and her mother keep her pre-Gilead identity alive.
Language in Gilead :: Biblical names (Rachel and Leah Centre), set greetings ('Blessed be the fruit') and banned words show control.
Aunt Lydia :: Trains Handmaids at the Red Centre: 'Freedom to and freedom from'.
